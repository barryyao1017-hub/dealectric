Why an OBD tracker sleeps when parked, and stops when unplugged

The tracker has no charger and no wiring because the OBD-II connector feeds it. CSS Electronics describes the connector this way: "Pin 16 supplies battery power (often while the ignition is off)". Since the port stays live in a parked car, the tracker does not switch off. It drops into a sleep mode and reports less often so it does not run down the car's 12 V battery.

Each maker sets that parked interval differently:

  • Spytec Pulse OBD. The Hapn user manual gives the default as "every 3 hours when not moving and every 2 seconds when moving". Hapn's reporting-mode table lists Performance (default) at 2 s moving and 3 h stopped, Normal at 15 s and 3 h, Eco at 30 s and 3 h, and Predictable, which lets you set the stopped interval to 1, 3, 6, 12 or 24 hours but turns trip reporting off. Spytec's guide by Patrick Icasas says that if the vehicle is moved while off, "the motion sensor will wake the device and alert you".
  • Bouncie. Its help centre says the device "enters into a low-power sleep mode" and checks in "every few hours". Where there is no cell signal, such as some garages, it makes a few attempts and then stops "in order to protect the vehicle battery while parked", so the map may show no fresh position until the next drive.
  • Vyncs. Its FAQ says: "Even when the engine is off, Vyncs will update its location every hour until the device is removed from the OBD-II port."

The same dependence on the port decides what happens when someone pulls the tracker out. Bouncie's help centre says "the alert is sent when the device is reinstalled and not when it's initially removed", because the device cannot reach its servers without power. Vyncs has no battery and notifies you the next time the device is plugged in. Spytec's guide says the Pulse OBD's internal backup battery lets it send one final "Power Disconnected" alert. Spytec's product page lists that battery as 60 mAh and the Hapn manual lists 115 mAh; either way it is sized for a last message, not for continued tracking.

Fit: CSS Electronics dates the US requirement for OBD2 on cars and light trucks to 1996. The Hapn manual adds that "not every vehicle model is supported, and some supported vehicles can't read all available metrics", and that Spytec's optional splitter cable "may interfere with the OBD Tracker's ability to read any telematic data from the vehicle". Battery-electric cars are the case to check before ordering: CSS Electronics says "electric vehicles are not required to support OBD2 in any shape or form", while Spytec's guide says EVs sold in the US still have the port. Look for the 16-pin socket under the dash first. Spytec lists US, Canada and Mexico coverage, and Bouncie's FAQ lists the United States including Puerto Rico, Canada and Mexico.

Not recommended: Vyncs Basic 4G+ for following a moving car

The "60 seconds" on the Basic plan is a GPS sample rate. Vyncs's own footnote says the points reach your account "together every 3 minutes", which is 3.25 miles of road at 65 mph. Speeding it up on Basic costs $30 a year for 20-second sampling plus $20 a year for live map auto refresh: $145.98 + $30 + $20 = $195.98 in year one. Vyncs Pro includes both for $165.98, which is $30.00 less, and Spytec Pulse OBD reports every 2 seconds for $107.40. Vyncs Basic remains a fair choice when the job is knowing where a parked car is, hour by hour.
