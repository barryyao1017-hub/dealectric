What the differences mean

Franklin sizes a backup source for a SubDrive Connect at 1.5 × the drive's maximum input watts. Dividing its minimums by 1.5 gives the most each drive draws:

  • SubDrive20: 5,700 W ÷ 1.5 = 3,800 W drawn at most. SubDrive30: 7,000 ÷ 1.5 = 4,667 W. SubDrive50: 11,000 ÷ 1.5 = 7,333 W.
  • Sun Gold 8KW: 8,000 ÷ 7,000 = 1.14× the SubDrive30 bar. It is the only single unit here that clears it. At $1,305 ÷ 8,000 W it costs $0.163 per continuous watt.
  • SPH6548P: 6,500 ÷ 5,700 = 1.14× the SubDrive20 bar, at $1,035 ÷ 6,500 W = $0.159 per watt, the lowest here.
  • 6,000 W units (Sun Gold 6000W, EG4 6000XP, AIMS): 6,000 ÷ 5,700 = 1.05×, a 5% margin on a SubDrive20. Per watt they cost $0.203 (Sun Gold), $0.25 (EG4) and $0.375 (AIMS).

Why a SubDrive changes the number that matters

A conventional well-pump motor connects straight to the line and takes a large burst of current as it starts, which is what an inverter's surge rating is for. A Franklin SubDrive works differently. Franklin's FAQ says the drive "starts and increases the motor speed slowly, gradually increasing voltage", which gives lower start-up current. The load the inverter must carry is then the drive's steady input, and Franklin's owner's manual turns that into a sizing rule: 1.5 × the drive's maximum input watts, rounded up to the next standard generator size. That gives minimums of 3,500 W for the SubDrive15, 5,700 W for the SubDrive20, 7,000 W for the SubDrive30 and 11,000 W for the SubDrive50. Franklin writes the rule for generators. It is the closest guidance Franklin publishes, so we apply it to each inverter's continuous rating.

Three more Franklin conditions narrow the field:

  • 240 V only. The drive needs 190–260 VAC between L1 and L2 and shows an F2 fault below 180 VAC. Every inverter in the table delivers 120/240 V split phase.
  • No GFCI. The manual says not to use the drive with a GFCI, and the FAQ warns that GFCI and AFCI breakers may cause nuisance trips. Check the inverter's output breaker or transfer panel for this.
  • Two-wire motors don't soft-start. In Franklin's words, "Two-wire motors do not have soft start ability." With a 2-wire pump, or with no drive at all, surge decides again.

When surge does decide, the published figures differ widely. AIMS rates its 6,000 W unit at 18,000 W for 20 s. Sun Gold rates the 8KW at 16,000 W peak and a 6 HP loaded motor, and the SPH6548P at 13,000 VA with no hold time. EG4 rates the 6000XP at 12,000 W for about 3.5 s. When The Solar Lab tested the 6000XP, it held 7.3–7.5 kW for 3–5 s (61–63% of 12,000 W, or about 31 A at 240 V), held 6.25 kW continuous before tripping, and started the testers' compressor. The Solar Lab also measured about 50 W of idle draw on the 6000XP. Over a day that is 50 × 24 = 1.2 kWh, which matters when the inverter sits waiting for a pump that runs a few minutes an hour. To size for a plain pump, compare the locked-rotor amps on the motor's nameplate with the surge rating divided by 240 V. For example, the AIMS unit's 18,000 W ÷ 240 V = 75 A.

Not recommended

  • AIMS PICOGLF60W48V240VS for a SubDrive system. Its 18,000 W, 20 s surge goes unused when the drive soft-starts the motor. Its 6,000 W continuous rating clears the same SubDrive20 bar as the Sun Gold 6000W, and it costs $1,028.86 more ($2,248.86 − $1,220).
  • A single Victron MultiPlus-II 48/5000/70-95 120V. It delivers 4,000 W (5,000 VA) continuous and 9,000 W peak, but only at 120 V. A SubDrive needs 190–260 VAC, so one unit can't run the drive.
