Where a plug-in battery can feed your wall outlet, and why that's rare

A battery can only lower your bill through a wall socket if its inverter is grid-interactive. EcoFlow's STREAM Ultra is listed to UL 1741 and IEEE 1547 and has built-in anti-islanding protection. EcoFlow's US product page says that, under Utah's H.B. 340, it is "only available for sale and plug-and-play installation in Utah." EcoFlow's state tracker, dated 2026-07-04, also names Maryland. A single Ultra exports at most 800 W. Reaching the 1,200 W cap that Utah and Maryland allow takes the separate STREAM Microinverter or 2–6 Ultra units. In an outage it powers only what is plugged into its two outlets, and each appliance must have a surge power below 1,200 W. EcoFlow says pairing with DELTA power stations "will be available soon." Without net metering, EcoFlow recommends a Shelly Pro 3EM meter so the system doesn't export to the grid. The product page does not list UL 3700, the new plug-in PV standard.

Why the caps exist: PV Tech reports that a plug-in system feeds current in below the breaker, which "could overload parts of the circuit without the upstream breaker detecting it." A portable power station avoids all of this. BLUETTI says its stations and portable panels "do not feed electricity into home wiring or the utility grid." No state cap or landlord wiring approval applies. You move the fridge, router or laptop to the station.

Not recommended

The BLUETTI Elite 200 V2 + 200 W kit is the cheapest per Wh here ($0.58), but its single 200 W panel needs about 10.4 h of full sun to refill 2,073.6 Wh. That's two sunny days for a balcony setup.
