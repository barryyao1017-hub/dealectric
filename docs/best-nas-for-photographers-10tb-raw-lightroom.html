What the differences mean

  • The network link sets the upper limit. One 2.5GbE port carries at most 2,500 Mb/s ÷ 8 = 312.5 MB/s. QNAP measured 295.31 MB/s on one, which is 94% of that. One 10GbE port carries at most 10,000 ÷ 8 = 1,250 MB/s.
  • Over 10GbE, the hard drives set the real speed. MightyGadget measured 500–600 MB/s from a RAID 5 array of WD Red Plus drives in the DXP4800 Plus. That is 500 ÷ 295 to 600 ÷ 295 = 1.7–2.0× a 2.5GbE port, not the 4× the port speeds suggest.
  • Copying a 100 GB shoot. 100,000 MB ÷ 550 MB/s = 182 s, about 3.0 min over 10GbE. 100,000 ÷ 295 = 339 s, about 5.6 min over 2.5GbE.
  • Loading the 10 TB library. 10,000,000 MB ÷ 295 MB/s = 33,900 s, about 9.4 h over 2.5GbE. At 550 MB/s it is 18,200 s, about 5.1 h on the DXP4800 Plus.
  • Synology's headline figure. 522 MB/s ÷ 312.5 MB/s = 1.67 links' worth, so the DS925+ rating needs both ports and several computers. Synology tested with all onboard ports and 8 clients. One Mac or PC on one cable gets one link.
  • Cost of 10GbE at the NAS. The DXP4800 Plus is $619.99 with 10GbE built in. The TS-464-8G is $639.00, plus $99.00 for a card, so $738.00 in total. That is $118.01 more, and QNAP listed the card with a 2–4 week lead time. The DS925+ cannot be upgraded to 10GbE at all. Your computer also needs a 10GbE port or adapter.

What one computer actually gets from each NAS

Makers measure throughput with several computers using every port at once. A photographer editing on one Mac or PC uses one link. QNAP's own lab results for the TS-464 show what one link gives: 295.31 MB/s read and write over one 2.5GbE port, and 1,181.04 MB/s read over one 10GbE port with a QNAP card (RAID 5 for 4-bay units, MTU 9000). QNAP's lab drives produced that 10GbE figure, not a home array of hard drives. With hard drives in RAID 5, MightyGadget measured the DXP4800 Plus at 500–600 MB/s and named the drives, not the network, as the limit.

File size changes the result. When copying 10,000 files of 1 MB each, QNAP measured 274.94 MB/s read over 10GbE. That is 23% of its large-file figure (274.94 ÷ 1,181.04). Over 2.5GbE it measured 177.98 MB/s. RAW files of tens of MB fall between the two cases. So 10GbE pays off when you import a shoot, build previews or export in batches. Scrolling and editing one image at a time depends mostly on the catalog and previews, which belong on the computer's SSD.

That catalog rule comes from Lightroom Classic: the catalog can't be on a network drive, but the photos can. Lightroom Classic 15.4 and 15.4.1 also could not save catalog backups to SMB NAS shares on macOS 26. Adobe's August 2026 release fixed this, so update Lightroom Classic before you point backups at the NAS. The cloud-based Lightroom app keeps its library in Adobe's cloud, and the Lightroom and Photography plans include 1 TB, a tenth of a 10 TB library. With that app, a NAS works as a backup target, not as the working library.
