What the differences mean

Runtime is the energy a station actually delivers as 120 V AC, divided by the heater's draw. On high, every 1 kWh unit here is within four minutes of the others. What decides between them is AC headroom, price and weight.

  • BLUETTI AC180: 950 Wh ÷ 1,500 W × 60 = 38 min. At $499 that is $499 ÷ 38 = $13.1 per minute of full heat. It is also the heaviest unit here: 36 lb (OutdoorGearLab).
  • BLUETTI Elite 100 V2: 890 ÷ 1,500 × 60 = 35.6 min. At the same $499 that is $14.0 per minute, but it weighs 25.0 lb (OutdoorGearLab), 11 lb less than the AC180.
  • EcoFlow DELTA 3 Classic: $459 ÷ 34.8 min = $13.2 per minute, using the DELTA 3's 870 Wh. It is the lowest outlay for a 1,800 W unit.
  • Headroom: 1,800 − 1,500 = 300 W on the BLUETTI and EcoFlow units; 2,000 − 1,500 = 500 W on the Anker; 1,500 − 1,500 = 0 W on the Jackery.
  • Heater on low (750 W): 950 ÷ 750 × 60 ≈ 76 min on the AC180.
  • Heated blanket: if its label says 100 W, 950 ÷ 100 ≈ 9.5 h on the AC180. Treat this as an upper figure, because the 950 Wh was measured at a high load and a small load also pays the inverter's idle draw.
  • Twice the time: BLUETTI Elite 200 V2, 2,080 Wh and 2,600 W, $899. 2,080 × 0.85 ÷ 1,500 × 60 ≈ 71 min. The 0.85 sits inside the 82–89% the labs measured above. That works out to $899 ÷ 71 = $12.7 per minute.

Measured, not assumed: what each station really delivers

The Wh on the label is the battery's capacity. Some of it is lost turning DC into 120 V AC, and the station also keeps a small reserve. Named labs drained these units and measured what came out:

  • OutdoorGearLab: AC180 950 Wh of 1,152 (82%), run at 80% load, which is 1,800 × 0.8 = 1,440 W, about a heater's draw. Jackery 1000 v2 910 Wh, Elite 100 V2 890 Wh, DELTA 3 870 Wh (85–87%; OutdoorGearLab does not give the load for these three).
  • StorageReview: AC180 1,030 Wh at a 350 W load (89%).
  • Notebookcheck: C1000 Gen 2 850 Wh (83%) at about 250 W on the 230 V EU unit.

Two tests ran at heater-sized loads. The Solar Lab ran the Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 at 1,479 W for 37.5 minutes, 87.5% of the 43 minutes its label implies (1,479 × 37.5 ÷ 60 ≈ 924 Wh). The Technology Man ran the Anker SOLIX C1000 Gen 2 on a constant 1 kW electric heater and got 907 Wh over just over 54 minutes. So a 1 kWh station gives a 1,500 W heater about 34–38 minutes, not the 40 that 1,000 ÷ 1,500 suggests.

The Jackery runs at its limit. The Solar Lab held it at 1,500–1,560 W for 10 minutes, and a 2,200 W surge shut it off within a couple of seconds. It will run a heater that draws 1,500 W or less. A heater that pulls slightly over its label, or a phone charger added to the same station, leaves it no margin. The Technology Man's UK C1000 Gen 2 overheated after 26 minutes on a 2 kW heater placed close to it. US outlets cannot supply that load, but the test still shows why you should keep the heater's airflow away from the station.

The 1,800 W units also list a higher mode: BLUETTI's 2,700 W Lifting Power and EcoFlow's X-Boost. EcoFlow describes X-Boost as reducing the output voltage. A heater's output falls with the square of the voltage (P = V² ÷ R), so a heater running in these modes gives less heat. A 1,500 W heater sits under the 1,800 W continuous rating and never needs them.

Not recommended

BLUETTI Elite 30 V2 for a space heater. BLUETTI's store lists it as '600W Rated Power, 1500W Lifting Power'. The 1,500 W is the voltage-lowering lifting mode, not continuous output, so a 1,500 W heater would run in reduced-heat mode. Even at the full 1,500 W, its 288 Wh would last 288 × 0.85 ÷ 1,500 × 60 ≈ 10 minutes. It suits small loads, not a heater.
