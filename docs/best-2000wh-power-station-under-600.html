What each 2 kWh station delivered at the outlet

The capacity on the box is the energy in the cells. What reaches an appliance is lower, because the inverter that makes 120 V AC uses power the whole time it is on. Techtest (Michael Barton) shows the size of that loss on the F2400: the same 100 W load drew 1,807 Wh over USB-C but 1,512 Wh from the AC outlet, a 295 Wh difference. Over the 15.1-hour AC run that is 295 ÷ 15.1 = about 19.5 W of extra loss with the inverter running. A light load is the worst case, since that overhead is a large share of 100 W.

FOSSiBOT's own FAQ sizes runtime as watt-hours × 0.85 ÷ device watts, so the maker's planning figure is 2,048 × 0.85 = 1,741 Wh, or 17.4 hours at 100 W. Both named tests came in below it: 1,512 Wh (Techtest) and 1,659 Wh (nextpit, Thomas Kern, load not stated). nextpit also metered 2,127 Wh from the wall for a full charge, so 1,659 ÷ 2,127 = 78% round trip, and a 2.5-hour charge against the maker's 2-hour claim. Techtest timed about 2 h 45 min at the 1,100 W setting.

The Anker SOLIX F2000 delivered about 1,800 Wh over a good 7 hours at roughly 250 W (Notebookcheck, Marcus Schwarten) and 1,870 Wh before inverter cutoff (StorageReview, Jordan Ranous). It took 2,300 to 2,309 Wh to refill, and Notebookcheck timed a full charge at just under 1.5 hours. The Jackery Explorer 2000 v2 gave 1,740 Wh in OutdoorGearLab's text and 1,710 Wh in its spec table (Chris McNamara and Kenji Mowrey), with a 2.5-hour charge. The BLUETTI Elite 200 V2 discharged 1.91 kWh and needed 2.44 kWh to recharge (StorageReview, Dylan Dougherty): 1.91 ÷ 2.44 = 78% round trip.

These tests used different loads and labs, and Techtest, nextpit and Notebookcheck tested 230 V European units, so read the gaps as indicative. The ranking still holds across them: the F2400 delivers the smallest share of its label of the four.
