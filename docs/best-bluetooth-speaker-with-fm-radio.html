What the differences mean

  • PowerByte stored energy: 7.4 V × 3.6 Ah = 26.6 Wh.
  • What the 20-hour claim draws: 26.6 Wh ÷ 20 h = 1.33 W average at 50% volume, which is 4.4% of the 30 W rating (1.33 ÷ 30).
  • Limit at the full rating: 26.6 Wh ÷ 30 W = 0.89 h, before amplifier losses. Music rarely holds full power for long, so this is the most it could manage at a constant 30 W, not what a loud afternoon will give you.
  • PowerByte vs SP850: 26.6 ÷ 3.7 = 7.2 times the energy.
  • PowerByte vs Studebaker SB2006: 3.6 Ah × 3.6–3.7 V = about 13 Wh, so the PowerByte holds about twice as much (26.6 ÷ 13.3 = 2.0).
  • Price per Wh: PowerByte $99.99 ÷ 26.6 = $3.76; Studebaker $59.99 ÷ 13.3 = about $4.50; SP850 $36.99 ÷ 3.7 = $10.00.
  • Plug-in draw: the Panasonic SC-PM270 uses 14 W in normal use. At that rate, a battery the size of the PowerByte's would last 26.6 ÷ 14 = 1.9 h, which is why it plugs into the wall.

Why 30 W and 20 hours can't happen at the same time

Runtime equals battery energy divided by average power draw. Avantree's PowerByte manual lists a built-in 7.4 V 3,600 mAh battery, 'Up to 20hrs (at 50% volume)' and 30 W (15 W × 2). Those 20 hours mean an average draw of about 1.3 W. Turn it up and the draw climbs toward the rating while the battery stays at 26.6 Wh, so the hours drop. Battery energy is the figure to compare across speakers, because a watt rating with no volume level or test condition can't be compared. The PowerByte stores the most energy of the speakers listed here, so it wins for long listening on battery at moderate volume. It is the wrong choice if you want it loud all afternoon away from a socket.

On the radio side, all five tune FM, and only the Studebaker and the Tivoli add AM. The PowerByte's manual lists 87–108 MHz, which covers US FM stations. On Avantree's BoomByte ($59.99 at Avantree), a 'Campus Band' setting limits the search to 76–90 MHz, according to Avantree Support. Switching to 'Common Band' restores 87–108 MHz. For digital radio, the US uses HD Radio, which HD Radio's own regulatory page says the FCC approved on 11 October 2002, not DAB. A DAB/DAB+/FM model such as the JBL Tuner 2 therefore works only as an FM radio in the US.
