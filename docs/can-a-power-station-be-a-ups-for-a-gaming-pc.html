Can a portable power station replace a UPS for a gaming PC, and which ones switch over fast enough? (2026)

Short answer. Yes, if the portable power station switches over in about 10 ms or less: the Anker SOLIX C1000 Gen 2 (price on the Amazon page; buy on Amazon: https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FN7MSY4L?tag=dealectric-20) measured under 10 ms on a reviewer's oscilloscope with a desktop running a graphics benchmark, which stayed on, and its 2,000 W output covers any gaming PC, with about 1.8 hours of gaming at 500 W (907 Wh delivered in the same test).

What to know: no one has published a pull-the-plug test with a named gaming PC. The 20 ms units are marginal for a gaming PC UPS (BLUETTI AC180, Jackery Explorer 1000 v2, EcoFlow RIVER 3): ATX 3.1 power supplies need only 12 ms of hold-up at full load, and AC180 owners report desktops dropping. For a clean save-and-shutdown, a CyberPower CP1500PFCLCD (4 ms, about 10 minutes at 500 W, $239.95–274.95) is the classic answer; the BLUETTI Elite 300 ($1,199; buy: https://dealectric.com/go/bluetti-elite-300, Dealectric-exclusive code DEALECTRIC saves $59.95) measured 8.9 ms and runs about 5 hours.

Why 10 ms is the line

When the grid drops, the PC keeps running on the energy stored in its power supply's capacitors until the station's inverter takes over. Seasonic's summary of the ATX rules: ATX 3.0 asks for 17 ms of hold-up at full load, ATX 3.1 relaxes it to 12 ms at full load and recommends 17 ms at 80 % load. Measured 750–1,000 W units land around 16–18 ms (hwbusters measured 17.6 ms on a Gigabyte AE1000PM and about 17 ms on a be quiet! Dark Power 14 1000 W; igor'sLAB over 17 ms on a Corsair RM850e), and the power-good signal drops a little earlier. A gaming load is not full load on most PSUs, which adds margin, but a 20 ms switch is longer than the hold-up of most supplies at any load, while 10 ms is inside it.

What the measurements show

StorageReview timed transfers with a Quarch module on a workstation: Jackery Explorer 2000 Plus 8.7 ms, a real Eaton 5PX G2 UPS 8.6 ms, BLUETTI AC60 and AC70 about 14 ms, UGREEN PowerRoam 1200 about 15.3 ms, Anker SOLIX F2000 about 27.4 ms against a 20 ms claim, EcoFlow DELTA 2 about 38.5 ms against under 30 ms. Its DELTA 3 Plus review measured just over 8 ms. The Technology Man measured the C1000 Gen 2 under 10 ms and the Elite 300 at 8.9 ms on an oscilloscope, each with a desktop running a graphics benchmark through the switch. AC180 owners on BLUETTI's forum report switch times outside its 20 ms spec and a desktop that went off in four short outages.

How long it lasts, and what it costs to leave on

A gaming PC under load draws about 350–650 W at the wall (KitGuru measured just under 510 W for an RTX 4080 Super system in Cyberpunk 2077 at 4K; LanOC 526 W peak for an RTX 4080 build), plus the monitor. The C1000 Gen 2 delivered 907 Wh at a steady 1 kW and the DELTA 3 Plus 910 Wh, so about 2.5 hours at 350 W, 1.8 at 500 W and 1.4 at 650 W, our arithmetic. Idle, the station's inverter draws about 17 W, roughly 150 kWh a year if it sits on UPS duty all year, and the battery is held near full; set a charge limit in the app where it has one. Use a pure sine wave unit (all of these are): one owner's gaming PC would not start on a modified-sine station.

Standby, not online

All of these are standby designs that switch on loss of grid, and in UPS mode the grid must supply more than the load (EcoFlow's manual). Online units with no switch at all exist at a higher price (the BLUETTI Apex 300 and the EcoFlow DELTA Pro Ultra's online outlets are described as 0 ms; we found no independent confirmation that either is double-conversion).
