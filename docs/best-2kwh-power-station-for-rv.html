What the differences mean

Idle draw is the power the inverter uses whenever its AC side is on, before the RV draws anything. If the station feeds your RV's shore cord all day, the inverter never switches off, so idle draw runs around the clock:

  • BLUETTI Elite 200 V2: 10 W × 24 h = 240 Wh a day; 240 ÷ 2,073.6 Wh = 11.6% of the battery.
  • EcoFlow DELTA 3 Max: 27 W × 24 h = 648 Wh; 648 ÷ 2,048 Wh = 31.6%.
  • Jackery Explorer 2000 v2: 17 W × 24 h = 408 Wh; 408 ÷ 2,042 Wh = 20.0%.
  • Anker SOLIX C2000 Gen 2, using Anker's own 9 W figure because no reviewer has published a measurement: 9 W × 24 h = 216 Wh; 10.5%.

Rough fridge runtime, using measured usable energy ÷ (60 W average fridge + idle draw): Elite 200 V2 1,910 ÷ 70 = 27 h; Anker 1,600 ÷ 69 = 23 h; Jackery 1,710 ÷ 77 = 22 h; DELTA 3 Max 1,618 ÷ 87 = 19 h. The measured usable energy already includes some inverter loss, so adding idle on top makes these conservative figures.

What a 30-amp RV plug really gets: a campground 30 A pedestal supplies 120 V × 30 A = 3,600 W. Anker rates its built-in TT-30R at 20 A max, 2,400 W. Each of the Elite 200 V2's four NEMA 5-20 outlets is rated 20 A (StorageReview), so a 5-20P-to-TT-30R adapter delivers 120 V × 20 A = 2,400 W: the same ceiling as Anker's built-in outlet. BLUETTI's box list for the Elite 200 V2 has no RV adapter, so buy one separately.

Idle draw: the number that drains an RV battery overnight

These are the figures named reviewers published with the AC inverter on and nothing plugged in. The Solar Lab calculated about 27 W for the EcoFlow DELTA 3 Max and about 17 W for the Jackery Explorer 2000 v2. The Gadgeteer's Greg Cleveland lists about 10 W for the BLUETTI Elite 200 V2 with AC and DC left on, and got about 2,000 Wh (over 93%) out of it. StorageReview's Dylan Dougherty discharged 1.91 kWh from the Elite 200 V2 and needed 2.44 kWh to refill it. For the Anker SOLIX C2000 Gen 2, Notebookcheck measured about 1,600 Wh at a 200 W load, or 79% of rated, on the EU model. Anker's 9 W idle figure is its own claim. A fridge is a small, constant load, which is where idle draw and low-load efficiency matter most. That is why the Elite 200 V2 ($899 on bluettipower.com, $854.05 through this link: https://dealectric.com/go/bluetti-elite-200-v2) wins for overnight RV use.

Two cautions apply to the Elite 200 V2 in an RV. First, its 3,900 W Power Lifting mode is for pure resistive loads only, by BLUETTI's own description. It is not surge headroom, so don't count on it to start a rooftop air-conditioner compressor. Second, many RV energy-management systems (EMS) and surge protectors refuse power from portable stations. Mike Sokol (RVelectricity) explains that BLUETTI, Jackery and similar stations leave neutral and ground unbonded, so a Progressive-type EMS reports an open ground. His fix is a 3-way splitter: a neutral-ground bonding plug in one socket and the RV cord in another.

The BLUETTI AC200L, the older pick with a built-in TT-30, is listed at $899 on BLUETTI's US store but is marked Discontinued and unavailable as of 2026-11-13.

Not recommended

The EcoFlow DELTA 3 Max is the cheapest 2 kWh station here at $879. As an overnight RV battery feeding a fridge through the inverter, though, its roughly 27 W idle draw (The Solar Lab) uses about 648 Wh a day, close to a third of the battery, before the fridge runs at all. The Solar Lab also lists only 500 W of solar input and took over four hours to recharge it from panels. Choose it for short daytime loads with the inverter switched off between uses, not for running an RV around the clock.
