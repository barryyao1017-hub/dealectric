What the differences mean

  • How long they last if you run them flat: 600 ÷ 150 = 4.0. Drained fully each time in Panasonic's test, a standard eneloop takes four times as many charges as an eneloop pro. The old shallow test gives almost the same ratio: 2,100 ÷ 500 = 4.2.
  • Runtime per charge, measured: Techtest.org (Michael Barton, March 2026) tested all three on the same bench at 0.5 A. Eneloop pro gave 2,441 mAh, LADDA 2450 gave 2,377 mAh and standard eneloop gave 1,862 mAh. LADDA against pro: 2,377 ÷ 2,441 = 97.4%, so 2.6% less. LADDA against eneloop: 2,377 ÷ 1,862 = 1.28, so about 28% more per charge.
  • Charge left after a year in a drawer: eneloop keeps 2,000 mAh × 90% = 1,800 mAh, and eneloop pro keeps 2,550 mAh × 85% = 2,168 mAh, using Panasonic's typical capacity and retention figures. After one year the pro still holds more. Panasonic gives a 10-year figure only for standard eneloop: 70%, about 1,400 mAh.
  • Energy you get for the money, per charge: Tenergy Premium Pro holds 1.2 V × 2.8 Ah = 3.36 Wh. At $42.99 ÷ 20 = $2.15 a battery, that is $0.64 per Wh. IKEA LADDA holds 1.2 V × 2.45 Ah = 2.94 Wh. At $11.99 ÷ 4 = $3.00 a battery, that is $1.02 per Wh. Tenergy's capacity is the maker's rating; Which? measured LADDA at its rated capacity.

Why the same eneloop is rated for both 2,100 and 600 cycles

Panasonic's Japanese site gives two cycle counts for each eneloop, one from each version of the Japanese standard JIS C 8708. The old 2013 test (clause 7.5.1.3) uses a small current and recharges the battery before it is used up. It gives about 2,100 cycles for eneloop and about 500 for eneloop pro, and those are the numbers still printed on US packs. The standard was revised in March 2019. The current 2019 test (clause 7.5.1.4) uses a large current and empties the battery before each recharge. It gives about 600 cycles for eneloop and about 150 for eneloop pro. Panasonic says the batteries themselves did not change, only the test.

Panasonic's Japanese page lists the Japanese models. Panasonic has not published full-drain figures for the US models, BK-3MCCA and BK-3HCCA. We apply the same 600 and 150 to them because Panasonic says the batteries did not change.

A 1,200-cycle claim that names no test can't be ranked against either eneloop figure. If you run batteries flat every time, as a flash or a toy does, expect hundreds of charges, not thousands. That is the use where eneloop pro's 150 applies, not its 500.

Not recommended

  • XTAR 1.5 V lithium in clocks, radios, bright flashlights or camera flashes. XTAR's own FAQ says it is unsuitable for these. Clocks run it down fast, and its circuit causes noise in radios. Flashlights of about 300 lumens and up draw about 2.5 A, and the battery cuts its output above 2–2.5 A. Camera flashes can trip its protection circuit. XTAR lists the devices it suits as game controllers, wireless mics, RC toys, trail cameras and multimeters.
  • Any rechargeable AA in a smoke alarm. First Alert says never to use rechargeable batteries in its alarms, because they may not give a consistent charge.
  • Rechargeables in a Kwikset smart lock, if you rely on its battery warning. Kwikset says its software is set up for alkaline batteries, so the battery percentage may be wrong and the low-battery warning may not come. Kwikset also says rechargeables will not harm the lock.
