What the differences mean

A music server stays on all day and night but spends most of that time idle, so its running cost comes from the power it draws while waiting. These yearly figures assume 4 hours of drive access and 20 hours with the drives asleep each day. They use the makers' own watts and the US average residential electricity rate of 18.16¢/kWh (EIA, August 2026).

  • DXP2800: (16.38 W × 4 h + 5.24 W × 20 h) × 365 = 62.17 kWh, × 0.1816 = $11.29 a year.
  • DS225+: (16.98 × 4 + 6.08 × 20) × 365 = 69.17 kWh = $12.56 a year.
  • AS5402T: (22.9 × 4 + 12.7 × 20) × 365 = 126.14 kWh = $22.91 a year.
  • TS-264: (29.086 × 4 + 18.288 × 20) × 365 = 175.97 kWh = $31.96 a year. Compared with the DXP2800: ($31.96 − $11.29) × 5 = $103.35 more over five years.

Why the makers' watts don't line up, and what reviewers measured

Each maker tests with a different drive setup. QNAP's footnote says its TS-264 figures were 'tested with drives fully populated', so both bays were filled. UGREEN's DXP2800 and DH2300 figures were taken with a single 2 TB hard drive (UGREEN lab figures, as listed by StarryHope). Synology's spec page refers readers to a separate method article, and Asustor's footnote names its test drive as a 16 TB Seagate IronWolf. Part of the gap between 5.24 W and 18.288 W therefore comes from the test setup, not the hardware.

Reviewer measurements with two drives keep the same order. ComputerBase tested the DXP2800 with two 20 TB HDDs at 18.6 W idle and 7.3 W with the drives spun down. In the same table, the DS224+ (the same Celeron J4125 and 2 GB platform as the DS225+) measured 18.6 W and 3.5 W. PCWorld measured the TS-264 at 26.5 W idle with two 4 TB IronWolf drives. LinuxLinks measured the AS5402T at 29.2 W with two 7,200 rpm drives plus two NVMe SSDs. The TS-264's idle draw is 7.9 W above the DXP2800's. Running all year, that is 7.9 W × 8,760 h = 69.2 kWh, or about $12.57 at 18.16¢/kWh. These tests came from different labs with different drives, so treat the gaps as approximate.

The Synology DS225+ is the honest alternative. ComputerBase measured its platform at less than half the DXP2800's draw with the drives spun down, so if your drives sleep most of the day it costs about the same to run. The DXP2800 still wins this question for two reasons. It ships with 8 GB of RAM against the DS225+'s 2 GB. And Plex calls Sonic Analysis 'very CPU-intensive', saying it can take 'hours or even multiple days'. Sonic Analysis needs an x86/x86-64 CPU, 64-bit Windows, macOS or Linux, Plex Media Server 1.24.0 or newer, and a Plex Pass on the server admin's account. You don't need a subscription for remote listening: Plex says its remote-streaming requirements 'apply only to movies/TV/video media' and do not affect music streamed to Plexamp. Navidrome publishes Docker images for amd64 and arm64, so any of these boxes that runs Docker can run it.

Not recommended

The UGREEN NASync DH2300 has the lowest running cost on paper, $8.02 a year at (13.72 × 4 + 3.304 × 20) × 365 = 44.15 kWh. It still fails both halves of this question. UGREEN's own Navidrome guide says the DH2300 does not support Docker, so it can't run Navidrome that way. Its Rockchip RK3576 is an ARM chip, and Plex says Sonic Analysis isn't available on ARM processors. Its low maker figure also didn't hold up in testing: NASCompares measured 9.5 W idle with two 4 TB IronWolf drives. For Navidrome and Plexamp, choose the x86 DXP2800.
