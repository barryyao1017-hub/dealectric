The sizing math

An e-bike charger is a 2–5 A supply at 36–54 V, so 110–220 W from the outlet; a maker's test of 48 V chargers measured 86–91 % efficiency (2 A best), and a power station's inverter returns 82–92 % of its rated watt-hours in independent discharge tests (AC180 950–1,030 Wh of 1,152; Elite 200 V2 1,910 of 2,073.6), likely toward the low end at a 100–200 W load. 700 Wh ÷ 0.88 ÷ 0.85–0.90 is about 880–935 Wh per full charge, so two charges need 1,800–1,900 Wh from the station: that is why a "2 kWh" station is the floor and a 1 kWh station is one charge. Rated watt-hours times 0.85 is the quick rule.

Things that stop a charge

  • Waveform: e-bike chargers want a pure sine wave; the Elite 200 V2, AC180 and S2000 Pro state pure sine (the others' spec pages were not readable to confirm).
  • ECO mode: BLUETTI's ECO setting switches AC off after hours under a low load (50 W for 4 hours on the AC200MAX, 40 W on the AC70P; the Elite 200 V2 and AC180 values are set in the app). A charger draws above those thresholds until the pack is full, then the station may power down, which is fine. The Elite 200 V2 also turns itself off after a minute with no load.
  • Nobody has tested a Bosch, Shimano, Rad, Lectric or Aventon charger on these stations in print; the compatibility case rests on pure sine and the inverter's headroom.

Refilling it

The Elite 200 V2 takes 96 W from a 12 V car socket (192 W at 24 V), so a nine-hour drive adds about one e-bike charge; its solar input is up to 1,000 W, and a measured run took it from 25 % to full between noon and 3:40 pm on 520–600 W of panels in July. With a 200 W panel in November sun, count on roughly one e-bike charge per clear day. The AC180's solar input is 500 W.

The cheaper answer for one bike

A spare battery (Lectric $350–575 regular, Aventon $499, Rad third-party packs $299–349) is one extra 700 Wh charge with no conversion loss, no weight beyond 3–4 kg and no inverter. A station only wins when it also runs a fridge, lights and phones, or charges two bikes.
