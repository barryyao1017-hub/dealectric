How much energy a 1 kWh station really gives

A station's rated Wh is the capacity of its cells. What reaches your appliance is lower, because the inverter, the battery management system and a reserve at the bottom of the pack all take a share. The figures below were measured by named labs running each station from full to empty:

Small loads come out worse. On a ~34 W grow light, Outdoor Life got 620 Wh from the AC180 over 15.1 h and 530 Wh from the DELTA 3 over 13.9 h, about half the rating. The inverter's own draw counts for more when the load is small.

Wiring the AC180 kit. BLUETTI rates the AC180's solar input at 500 W max, 12–60 V Voc and 10 A. Two 200 W panels in parallel run at a single panel's voltage, which is well under the 40 V needed, so the 10 A cap holds them below 400 W. Wired in series, the voltages add and the pair can deliver its full rating. Keep the series total under 60 V Voc, and use two panels of the same model. If you take the cheaper route ($769 on bluettipower.com, $730.55 through this link: https://dealectric.com/go/bluetti-ac180-200w-solar-kit), BLUETTI's variant listing doesn't name the kit's 200 W panel, so check that it is an SP200L before adding a second one.

The Anker kit. Anker's US page lists the C1000 Gen 2 + 400 W bifacial bundle as shipping from early December, but showed no price on 2026-11-08, so it isn't in the priced table. The C1000 Gen 2 takes 14.5 A only from 29–60 V. Below 29 V it takes 8.2 A, which caps a panel at 28 V × 8.2 A = 230 W. A 400 W panel therefore has to run at 29 V or more to deliver its full output.
