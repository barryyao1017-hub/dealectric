What the differences mean

The line for this page is the watt figure in the question: every row is one listing at one price, with panels in it, rated 4,000 W or more of continuous AC output. Dealectric does not test units; the figures below are makers' specifications, arithmetic on them, and one named reviewer's measurement.

  • Minutes of a 4,000 W load returned per hour of full sun = panel watts ÷ 4,000 W × 60. 600 W gives 9 minutes, 800 W gives 12, 400 W gives 6, 200 W gives 3. Read the other way, one hour at 4,000 W uses 4,000 Wh, which is 4,000 ÷ 600 = 6.7 hours of full sun on the six-panel BLUETTI kit and 4,000 ÷ 800 = 5 hours on the Anker kit.
  • Full sun to refill = battery Wh ÷ panel W at rated output: 5,120 ÷ 600 = 8.5 h, 5,024 ÷ 800 = 6.3 h, 5,120 ÷ 400 = 12.8 h, 5,120 ÷ 200 = 25.6 h, 5,040 ÷ 400 = 12.6 h. Real panel output is lower, so real refills take longer.

What the included panels do for a 4,000 W generator

The watt figure on a solar generator is what its inverter can draw from the battery. The panels only refill the battery, and in every kit here they are a small fraction of the output: 800 W is 20% of a 4,000 W load, 600 W is 15%, 400 W is 10% and 200 W is 5%. None of these kits runs a 4,000 W load on sun alone. At that load the battery lasts about 75 to 77 minutes and the panels stretch it by 3 to 12 minutes per hour of full sun.

One measurement exists for a kit of this kind. Andrew Riha of pv magazine USA (2026-10-28) wired two Anker SOLIX PS400 panels, 800 W rated, in series to the S5000's high-voltage input on a sunny October day and saw a 680 W peak, 85% of the rating. He wrote: "The panels, not the input, set that limit." At 680 W the S5000 refill becomes 5,024 ÷ 680 = 7.4 hours of peak sun instead of 6.3, and each such hour returns 680 ÷ 4,000 × 60 = 10 minutes of a 4,000 W load. Anker's kit page does not name the panel model in the two-panel bundle, so read this as the nearest measured case. In the same review the S5000 delivered 4.87 kWh on a full discharge (read from Anker's app, not an external meter) and took 6.24 kWh from the wall to recharge, a 78% round trip.

The S5000 has room for more panels later: Anker lists two solar inputs, 1,000 W at 11–60 V and 2,000 W at 16–165 V, so the kit's 800 W is 27% of the 3,000 W it accepts. StormWatts gives the PS400 an open-circuit voltage of 57.6 V; two in series are 115.2 V, which fits only the 16–165 V input.

One more point on the Anker's watt figure: its FAQ caps a 120 V NEMA 5-20R outlet at 2,400 W and the 120 V TT-30R at 3,600 W, and gives the full 4,500 W only at the 240 V L14-30R. A single 4,000 W appliance on the S5000 therefore has to be a 240 V appliance. The same FAQ says the S5000 takes only BP5000 expansion batteries and does not work with Anker's Home Power Panel; it connects to a house through a transfer switch or power inlet box.

The BLUETTI pick is a different kind of product. The box holds the RV5 Power Hub, a B4810 battery, the Epanel and Epad, a drilling template, screws and wiring packs, and BLUETTI's FAQ says most RV owners can install it with basic DIY skills. It charges from solar, an alternator, shore power, a generator or an EV charger. It suits a vehicle or cabin wall, not a unit to roll out of the garage in an outage.
