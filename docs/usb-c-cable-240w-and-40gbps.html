How a cable tells the charger it can carry 240 W and 40 Gbps at once

Before a USB PD 3.1 charger raises the voltage above 20 V, it asks the cable to identify itself. Granite River Labs, a USB test lab, sets out the rule: the cable's e-marker chip "must be set to EPR Mode Capable and provide notification that it supports 50V and 5A." The USB Type-C spec, as quoted by CNX Software, says every EPR cable shall be electronically marked as 50 V and 5 A capable. The same chip also reports the cable's data speed, so a cable that does both carries both ratings in one ID. ChargerLAB's POWER-Z tester read LG's 240 W USB4 cable as 50 V 5 A EPR and USB4 Gen3 40 Gb. ChargerLAB measured 0.79 V of voltage drop on that cable at 28 V 5 A.

Length is where the two ratings split. Plugable says a passive Thunderbolt 4 cable is typically 1 m at most, and many are 0.8 m. Passive Thunderbolt 5 cables reach 1 m, and 2 m needs active retimer chips. Plugable's own active 2 m Thunderbolt 4 cable is rated 100 W. Apple's Thunderbolt 4 Pro Cable runs Thunderbolt 4 and USB4 at up to 40 Gbps but "delivers a maximum of 100W". Apple's 1 m Thunderbolt 5 Pro Cable delivers up to 240 W. So for any cable over 1 m, a 40 Gbps rating is not enough: the maker has to state 240 W or EPR.

On the packaging, Granite River Labs notes that a USB-IF certified 40 Gbps cable must also list its wattage, and that wattage can only be 60 W or 240 W. The mark you want reads 40Gbps and 240W. A 60 W mark means the e-marker will not report EPR, and the charger will not go above 20 V.

Not recommended

Plugable TBT4-40G2M (2 m, active): at $69.95 it costs twice as much as the 1 m 240 W cables and is rated 100 W, so it fails the 240 W half of the job. Apple Thunderbolt 4 Pro Cable: it carries 40 Gbps, but Apple caps it at 100 W. Both carry a Thunderbolt logo, which is why they catch shoppers out.
