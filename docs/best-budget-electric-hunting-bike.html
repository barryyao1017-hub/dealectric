What the differences mean

Hauling a stand and pack usually means riding in the top assist level, so the test that matters is the full-assist run. Reach to a stand assumes you ride out and back and keep 30% of the battery in reserve.

  • XPeak 2.0 Long-Range: 960 Wh ÷ 46.1 mi = 20.8 Wh per mile. Reach is 0.7 × 46.1 ÷ 2 = 16.1 mi one way.
  • Aventure 3: 733 Wh ÷ 35 mi = 20.9 Wh per mile, about as efficient as the XPeak. With the smaller pack, reach is 0.7 × 35 ÷ 2 = about 12 mi one way.
  • XPeak 2.0, 720 Wh: on OutdoorGearLab's hilly run, 720 Wh ÷ 24.6 mi = 29.3 Wh per mile. Reach is 0.7 × 24.6 ÷ 2 = 8.6 mi one way. This was a harder course than Electric Bike Report's, so it is a floor, not a like-for-like comparison.
  • Himiway D5 Pro: 960 Wh ÷ 28 mi = 34.3 Wh per mile while hill-carving. Reach is 0.7 × 28 ÷ 2 = 9.8 mi one way on steep ground.
  • Cost of the bigger battery: $1,599 − $1,299 = $300 more than the 720 Wh Step-Thru, or $1,599 − $1,399 = $200 more than the 720 Wh High-Step, for 240 Wh more. A second 960 Wh XPeak pack is $525 if you need more later.
  • Price per mile of reach, same reviewer and same top-assist test: XPeak Long-Range $1,599 ÷ 16.1 mi = $99 per mile. Aventure 3 $1,799 ÷ 12.25 mi = $147 per mile.
  • Cheapest battery capacity: a Kingbull Hunter 2.0S plus its $429 spare 18 Ah pack is $949 + $429 = $1,378 for about 1,728 Wh, or $0.80 per Wh. The XPeak Long-Range works out to $1,599 ÷ 960 = $1.67 per Wh. No reviewer has published a range test for the Hunter 2.0S.

How far a loaded bike gets you to the stand

Range claims are usually set in a low assist level. Gear, hills and throttle use push a bike into its top level, and that roughly halves range. Electric Bike Report ran its tests at full assist. The XPeak 2.0 (960 Wh) went 46.1 mi at PAS 5 and 65 mi at PAS 2. The Aventure 3 went about 35 mi in Turbo. Electric Bike Report scales the XPeak result to an estimated 34.6–48.8 mi on the standard 720 Wh pack. OutdoorGearLab's harder hilly run on that pack gave 24.6 mi.

Cold mornings. Aventon's cold-weather guide cites a capacity loss of at least 20% at 32°F. Applied to the XPeak's test, a 20% loss gives 46.1 × 0.8 = 36.9 mi, and reach drops to 0.7 × 36.9 ÷ 2 = 12.9 mi one way. Lectric says to charge only between 32°F and 104°F and to ride between −4°F and 113°F. A battery left in the truck overnight needs to warm up indoors before it can be charged. Lectric sells an XPeak thermal sleeve for $29.

Quiet. No reviewer has published a decibel reading for these bikes. Electric Bike Report's Griffin Hales called the XPeak's motor "seriously quiet". OutdoorGearLab's Joshua Hutchens found it "isn't noticeably quieter than other bikes in our test." Expect tire hum and rattling cargo to matter as much as the motor.

Steep climbs. The Himiway D5 Pro is the only mid-drive here. It drives the chain through the gears, with a 130 Nm rating on webBikeWorld's spec sheet, and is the one to look at if your stand sits up a steep grade and you would rather shift than lean on the throttle: $1,999 on himiwaybike.com, $1,949 through this link: https://dealectric.com/go/himiway-d5-pro. Expect fewer miles per charge on that terrain: webBikeWorld got 28 mi from 960 Wh while hill-carving.

Not recommended

Velotric Nomad 2 for long hauls. In Electric Bike Report's full-assist test it went 23.6 mi on 706 Wh. That is 29.9 Wh per mile, 44% more than the XPeak's 20.8 (29.9 ÷ 20.8 = 1.44), and leaves a reach of 0.7 × 23.6 ÷ 2 = 8.3 mi one way. Its 1,000 lb towing rating is Velotric's figure only. Electric Bike Review said it "remains unverified without a trailer in hand." Buy it for its comfort, not to tow gear into the woods.
