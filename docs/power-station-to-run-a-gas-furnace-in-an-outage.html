What to know: a single-speed PSC blower draws about 450–900 W running and 600–2,000 W to start, an ECM blower 90–300 W, plus 200–800 W for the inducer and 50–200 W for ignition and controls at start-up, so a furnace starts at 1,000–3,000 W and read your own nameplate (amps × 120 V); blowers run 8–15 minutes a cycle, two to four cycles an hour, up to 35–40 minutes an hour in the cold. Whether an 1,800 W inverter rides through a 2,000 W PSC start is undocumented, so the AC180 and P1800 are not furnace picks; BLUETTI's "Power Lifting" is for resistive loads, not motors. EcoFlow's DELTA Pro 3 ($2,799) and Anker's SOLIX F3800 are sold with transfer-switch and home-panel bundles that need professional installation; a 2–3 kW dual-fuel generator is the traditional answer and needs the same transfer switch.

The only safe connection

Reliance's TF-series manual says transfer switches "are required for use with portable generators by Article 702 of the National Electrical Code", that installation "must be performed by a qualified electrician, or others knowledgeable of electrical systems, in compliance with all applicable electrical codes", and that the furnace-mounted TF151/TF201 "should be connected by a licensed electrician or heating contractor" (the W variants mount at the panel with a conduit whip). The switch puts a GEN/LINE toggle on the furnace circuit and a flanged inlet (never a male plug) takes the cord from the station; the utility side of the main breaker stays live, so nobody should open the panel but the electrician. Some inspectors accept converting the furnace to cord-and-plug with a proper receptacle; whether NEC 422.16 allows that for a furnace is argued on every trade forum and decided by your local authority, so ask. Also ask the electrician and the furnace maker about polarity and grounding: furnace control boards sense flame through the ground path, and power stations commonly leave neutral floating; BLUETTI's pages show only a grounding screw with no guidance, and Anker's F3800 FAQ says neutral and ground "must be properly connected" when feeding an inlet box.

The arithmetic

PickHVAC's tables put PSC single-speed blowers at 500–900 W (text: 550–800 on average), ECM single-stage at 250–300 W and modulating ECM at 90–200 W, with the blower over 90 % of the furnace's electricity; PickComfort puts starting draws at 600–2,000 W (PSC), 200–800 W (ECM), 200–800 W (inducer) and 50–200 W (ignition and controls), total 1,000–3,000 W. No manufacturer nameplate, Energy.gov figure or measured station test was retrievable, and the hot-surface-igniter wattage is unsourced here, so the honest instruction is the nameplate and a clamp meter. Our table assumes 15 W of station idle, a 40 % duty cycle and 85 % usable energy: 12 hours of a 600 W PSC furnace is about 3.1 kWh, of a 300 W ECM furnace about 1.6 kWh.

The alternatives

EcoFlow sells the DELTA Pro 3 with a transfer switch or a Smart Home Panel 2 that "requires professional installation", and Anker markets the F3800's 240 V port for inlet boxes; both are whole-home routes at $2,800 and up before the electrician. A 2–3 kW dual-fuel inverter generator costs far less per kilowatt-hour over a multi-day outage, needs fuel, 20 feet of clearance and a carbon-monoxide alarm, and still needs the same transfer switch. If the house has a wood or pellet stove, that is the cheaper heat; the station then runs the stove and the fridge.
