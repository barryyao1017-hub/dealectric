What the differences mean

A car's accessory socket is a fused 12 V supply, and the charger has to pull its whole output through it. Mazda's 2024 MX-5 manual caps the socket at 120 W (10 A); Honda's 2011 CR-V manual allows 180 W (15 A). The headline watt figures only matter inside those limits.

  • A 100 W laptop on the UGREEN 150W: 100 W ÷ 0.865 (techtest.org's lowest measured efficiency) ÷ 12 V = 9.6 A. That fits a 10 A socket with little margin and a 15 A socket easily.
  • The full 150 W: 150 W ÷ 0.865 ÷ 12 V = 14.4 A, which is why UGREEN prints 18 A on the spec sheet and asks for a 20 A fuse.
  • Anker Nano 167.5W: 167.5 W ÷ 12 V = 14.0 A before any conversion loss, so a 10 A socket can never feed its rating.
  • Anker Nano 75W: 75 W ÷ 12 V = 6.25 A before losses; the label says 8 A. It is the highest-output candidate that fits a 10 A socket on its own rating.
  • Hold time: about 60 min for the UGREEN 150W against 6.6 min for the Anker 167.5W is roughly nine times longer at full laptop load.

Measured hold at full load

A 12 V car charger has to step 12 to 14 V up to 20 V and dissipate its losses inside a plug the size of a thumb, so heat, not the rating, decides how long a laptop actually charges. Michael Barton at techtest.org ran each charger at a constant load on a 12 V system (a Framework 16 laptop and an electronic load in the Anker and Baseus tests) and timed how long the full output lasted.
