Why cost per Wh and Wh per pound decide it

The capacity on the label is the battery's rating. Not all of it reaches your appliance. OutdoorGearLab got 84% of rated out of the 2000 v2 and 85% out of the 1000 v2, and HOBOTECH got 88% out of the 5000 Plus. Dividing the store price by rated Wh shows what each unit charges you for stored energy. Dividing Wh by weight shows how much you carry for that energy. On both counts the Explorer 2000 v2 comes out ahead of every 1.5 kWh-plus Jackery in the table. The 1500 Ultra charges a premium for weatherproofing, not for energy. The 5000 Plus has the lowest Wh per pound in the table, because you are paying for its 240 V inverter and 4,000 W solar input.

Switchover time matters if you plan to use the station as a UPS for a PC. An ATX 3.1 power supply only has to ride through 12 ms of lost AC at full load (Seasonic). Jackery rates both HomePower 1000 models at under 10 ms, which is inside that window. The Explorer 1000 v2 (20 ms, Notebookcheck) and the Explorer 2000 v2 (under 20 ms, Jackery) are not. For a fridge or lights, the gap makes no difference.

Idle draw matters in a long outage with small loads. With the inverter on, the 5000 Plus burns 58–85 W doing nothing, and in The Solar Lab's test it struggled to start an air conditioner with a 10,000 W start-up surge. For a CPAP, router and fridge over several days, another brand wins: Andrew Riha for pv magazine measured about 10 W idle on the Anker SOLIX S5000 (pv magazine) $2,499.99: https://dealectric.com/go/anker-solix-s5000.

Not recommended

Explorer 1000 Plus, if you're buying it to expand later. It ties the 2000 v2 at $0.49 per Wh, but Jackery's own product page says the Battery Pack 1000 Plus has been discontinued. That leaves its expand-to-5 kWh claim with no pack to buy, and it is rated for 4,000 cycles, against 6,000 on the HomePower v2 models.
