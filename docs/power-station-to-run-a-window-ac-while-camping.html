The arithmetic, and why the AC matters more than the station

A conventional 5,000 BTU unit is on or off: 450 W while the compressor runs, and in a hot tent it runs most of the night, so eight hours is up to 3.6 kWh plus the station's idle draw. An inverter unit (LG's 6,000 BTU dual inverter at CEER 15.5, Midea's U-shaped line) throttles the compressor instead of cycling it, so once the space is cool it sits at a fraction of its rating; at an assumed 50 % of 450 W plus 15 W idle, the night is about 1.9 kWh, which is what makes the Elite 300 a yes and the Elite 200 V2 a maybe. Start surge is two to three times running watts (BLUETTI and ALLPOWERS both quote about 1,200 W for a 500 W unit), inside every station here. No one has published a measured overnight run, so the honest instruction is a $20 plug-in meter on your own unit for one night at home.

Tents, campers and the purpose-built option

A window unit needs a wall: campers and vans can frame one in, tents mostly cannot, which is why the EcoFlow Wave 3 (a portable with a vent hose and its own 1,024 Wh battery) and the Zero Breeze Mark 3 exist; both claim a night only in low-power modes and cost $1,400–1,900 with a battery, and neither is a brand we carry. Weight matters too: the Elite 300 is a two-hand carry at 26.3 kg, the R4000 rolls at 45 kg and lives in a vehicle.

Refilling for night two

Two kilowatt-hours back into the station takes about 600 W of panel over a full sunny day (400 W × 4–5 sun-hours × 75 % is only 1.2–1.5 kWh); the Elite 300 accepts 1,200 W of solar, the R4000 2,000 W, the Elite 200 V2 1,000 W. A car socket at about 100 W adds a few hundred watt-hours a day and is not a plan; shore power at a campsite is.
