Feeding a 12 V NAS from a van battery

A 12 V LiFePO4 house bank sits at about 13.3 V full, charges at 14.2–14.6 V and an alternator can swing 12–15 V, while lead-acid sags toward 11.8 V when low, so a device built for a regulated 12 V brick should get an isolated DC-DC regulator (Victron Orion-Tr 12/12-9, 9 A at about 12.2 V, $64–75 in US listings on 2026-11-03) sized above the adapter's amps: at least 5 A for the DXP2800 and 6 A for the DH2300 until you read your adapter's label. A 19 V unit needs a 12-to-19 V boost module (8 A / 152 W modules run $18–64, input 9–16 V) and a 20 V unit a 20 V equivalent; a pure-sine inverter adds 3–8 W of no-load draw plus conversion loss to a 10–20 W NAS, which is why the 12 V boxes are the ones to buy.

The arithmetic

Amp-hours a day at 12 V are watts × 2: 3.6 W (Pi 5) is about 7 Ah, 10 W about 20 Ah, 15 W about 30 Ah and 25 W about 50 Ah, plus 5–15 % for the regulator. On a 100 Ah LiFePO4 bank a DH2300 idling all day takes a fifth of it; a DXP480T Plus a third; a DXP4800 Plus under load half. UGREEN's US3000 NAS UPS (12 V 10 A out) is listed for all four models but UGREEN does not say how it feeds the 19 V and 20 V units, so it is not a 12 V-rail answer.

Heat and vibration

UGREEN rates the DXP2800 for 0–40 °C (one retailer lists 35 °C maximum); a closed van cabinet in summer passes that, so give the NAS airflow. 3.5-inch NAS drives list roughly 30–70 G operating shock for 2 ms and 250–350 G non-operating, enough for a parked van and not a promise for a corrugated road; park the drives (hibernate) while driving or use SSDs. No published report measures a UGREEN, Synology or Pi NAS living in a van, so the figures here are bench numbers.
