Those medians come from the ENERGY STAR certified-products dataset (4,853 models, October 2026) as compiled by a secondary site; your own fridge's yellow EnergyGuide label gives its kWh per year, and a fridge over ten years old can use twice the median. The compressor draws about 100–250 W while running (about 190 W is typical) with a start surge of several hundred to about 1,200 W, running 30–50% of the time at room temperature and 80–90% in a 90 °F room (BLUETTI's figures). BLUETTI's own runtime calculator assumes a 300 W fridge at 40%, about twice the ENERGY STAR medians, so its 13.8 hours for the Elite 200 V2 is the worst case, not the typical one.

The arithmetic

Usable energy is the rated watt-hours times about 85% for the inverter. Divide by the fridge's daily kWh for hours of cover, and subtract the inverter's own idle draw (Anker's illustration uses 15 W, about 360 Wh a day) for the honest figure: a 1 kWh station gives 11–17 hours, 1.5 kWh 16–25, 2 kWh 22–35, 3 kWh 33–51. Measured runs are rare: Bob Vila's 30-plus hours on the Elite 200 V2 (fridge size unstated) is the one named-outlet result, consistent with a 1.6 kWh/day fridge. A 200–400 W folding panel in winter sun returns roughly 300–1,200 Wh a day, so a 400 W panel on a clear day can replace most of what the fridge takes.

Caveats

  • Surge: a station must start the compressor, not just run it; 2,000 W continuous and 3,000 W-plus surge covers every household fridge here.
  • Door shut: an unopened fridge holds safe temperatures for about 4 hours and a full freezer about 48 hours even with no power, so the station can be plugged in on a schedule rather than continuously.
  • Heat: a hot kitchen can halve the runtime; a garage fridge in summer is the worst case.
  • Check it: run the setup for a few hours with a plug-in watt meter, including at least one compressor restart, before the outage you are planning for.
