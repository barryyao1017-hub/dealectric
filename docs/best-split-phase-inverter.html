Split phase means two 120 V legs: check each one

A US split-phase inverter feeds your panel two 120 V legs, L1 and L2, with 240 V between them. The headline wattage is the 240 V total. Most house circuits are 120 V, though, and each one hangs on only one leg. If the lights, fridge, microwave and outlets mostly sit on L1, that leg can hit its limit long before the total does.

  • EG4 18kPV: EG4's manual says the inverter "can withstand up to 8kW of imbalance between L1 and L2", and it also advises keeping loads as balanced as possible. That leaves a lot of room for a typical, lopsided home panel.
  • EG4 6000XP: EG4's manual rates the output at 25 A at 240 V. SolarTech (Jonathan Richter) states a "3000W maximum per 120V leg" and says the 6000XP "cannot automatically balance loads between legs". So 4,000 W on one 120 V leg shuts it down even though the total is under 6,000 W.
  • Sol-Ark 15K/18K and Sun Gold SPH6548P: the store listings for these give the 240 V total only. Before wiring a panel with uneven loads, ask the seller for the L-N (120 V) continuous rating.

Before you buy, add up the 120 V loads you expect to run at once on each leg, not just the whole-house total. If either leg comes out above 3,000 W, one 6000XP will shut down. You would need to move circuits between legs, add a second unit, or step up to the 18kPV.

Battery note for the pick: EG4's 10-year 18kPV warranty excludes "damages incurred from a voltage or current spikes due to open-loop lithium battery communications", so pair it with a battery that talks closed-loop with the inverter.

Not recommended

A single EG4 6000XP for a panel full of 120 V circuits. Its 6 kW label works out to 3,000 W per 120 V leg (25 A × 120 V). It can't shift load between legs, and it can't export to the grid. In a house where most circuits are 120 V, it behaves like a 3 kW inverter on whichever leg is busiest.
