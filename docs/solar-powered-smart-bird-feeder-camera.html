What the differences mean

Refill hours are the battery's energy divided by the panel's rated watts. To get the energy we assumed a 3.7 V lithium-ion cell, because none of these makers states the cell voltage. The table also shows mAh per panel watt, which doesn't depend on that assumption. Charging losses are ignored, so real refill times are longer.

  • Kiwibit Bird Feeder 2: 5,200 mAh × 3.7 V = 19.2 Wh, and 19.2 Wh ÷ 4.4 W ≈ 4.4 h.
  • Kiwibit 4K Solar Panel Kit: 19.2 Wh ÷ 3 W ≈ 6.4 h.
  • Birdfy Feeder Wood: 19.2 Wh ÷ 2 W ≈ 9.6 h.
  • Birdfy Feeder 2 Pro: 9,000 mAh × 3.7 V = 33.3 Wh, and 33.3 Wh ÷ 3 W ≈ 11.1 h.
  • Birdfy Feeder Metal 2 4K: 33.3 Wh ÷ 2 W ≈ 16.7 h.
  • Sunny days: makers ask for 4–6 h of direct sun a day (BirdFeederLab). That makes 16.7 h ÷ 4–6 h ≈ 3–4 days for the Metal 2 4K and 4.4 h ÷ 4–6 h ≈ 1 day for the Kiwibit.
  • Reserve: 9,000 ÷ 5,200 ≈ 1.73, so the two 9,000 mAh Birdfys store about 73% more charge to get through days when the panel can't keep up.

How fast the sun refills each feeder, and when it stops

Every solar panel on a bird feeder is a top-up charger, not a fast charger. Birdfy's support FAQ says its panels are 'primarily designed to maintain your device's battery level, not to charge it from 0% to 100% quickly'. Notebookcheck's Stephen Pereyra calls even the Kiwibit's 4.4 W roof 'more of a charge maintainer'. That's why the panel-to-battery ratio matters: the less battery each panel watt has to refill, the sooner one sunny day makes up for a busy day of video clips.

The Gadgeteer's Vincent Nguyen ran the battery of a Kiwibit Bird Feeder 2 down to about 30%, mounted it in a Texas backyard in April, and found it 'back above 85%' within two full days of sun. After that it held between 70% and 95% on mostly sunny days. That works out to about 55% of 19.2 Wh, or 10.6 Wh, over two days: about 2.4 h at the panel's full rated output. Real output is well below the label, so treat the refill hours above as best cases. He also warns that frequent 3-minute 4K clips can drain the battery in under a week even with solar. MacRumors' Juli Clover saw where you mount the feeder matter as much as which model you buy. Her Birdfy feeder in a sunny spot 'never dipped below a 90 percent charge', while a second Birdfy in a wooded spot sat at about 50 percent.

Cold stops solar charging. Birdfy's FAQ says solar charging needs an air temperature of 32°F to 104°F. Kiwibit's winter guide, written around its Beako feeder, says its panel 'will not charge the battery below 0°C'. Both feeders keep working below freezing: the Birdfy Feeder 2 Pro to 14°F and the Kiwibit Bird Feeder 2 to −4°F. Through a freezing spell, then, the battery only drains, however sunny it is. In that weather battery size matters more than panel watts. Below 14°F only the Kiwibit is still within its rated range.
