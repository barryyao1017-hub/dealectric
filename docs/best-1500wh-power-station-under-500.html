What the differences mean

The watt-hours on the box are the energy in the battery pack. What reaches the AC outlets is less, because the inverter takes a share. The BLUETTI AC180 is the station here with the most published outlet tests, so its results set the range used for the estimates in the table.

  • Measured share of the label: 943 ÷ 1,152 = 82% (Ken Rockwell) and 1,030 ÷ 1,152 = 89% (StorageReview).
  • A 1,536 Wh label at that share: 1,536 × 0.82 = 1,260 Wh and 1,536 × 0.89 = 1,367 Wh. This is an estimate for the DYNESS, the Dynesspower PPS1800 and the Jackery, not a test of them.
  • PECRON E1500LFP: 1,536 × 0.90 = 1,382 Wh, using the 90% efficiency The Solar Lab reports.
  • 1,500 Wh at the outlet: 1,500 ÷ 0.89 = 1,685 Wh rated at best, 1,500 ÷ 0.82 = 1,829 Wh at worst. No station in the table is that large, so read 1500Wh as a label size.
  • Stepping up from the AC180 to the Pecron: 1,536 − 1,152 = 384 Wh more for $549 − $499 = $50, about $0.13 per added Wh at maker-store prices on 2026-11-11.
  • Maker-store check: of the four 1,536 Wh stations in the table, all priced on 2026-11-11, the Amazon DYNESS listing was the one below the cap. Dynesspower's own store has the same-spec PPS1800 at $549.99, level with the Pecron at $549.
