What the differences mean

  • Winter solar a day: panel watts × peak sun hours × 0.75. That is Eco Solar Kits' formula, and the page gives 2–3.5 sun hours for a northern winter. One 200 W panel: 200 × 2.5 × 0.75 = 375 Wh. Two panels: 400 × 2.5 × 0.75 = 750 Wh.
  • Summer: at 4 sun hours, 400 × 4 × 0.75 = 1,200 Wh. That covers Eco Solar Kits' 1,020 Wh hobby-workshop day: four LED shop lights, a 1,400 W saw for 15 minutes, a tool charger, a radio and a fan.
  • Bundle against parts: station $899 + two panels $798 = $1,697. The bundle is $1,499, which saves $198. Adding the $29 cable brings the kit to $1,528.
  • Wiring: two panels in series give 2 × 24.6 V = 49.2 V open-circuit, under the Elite 200 V2's 60 V limit. A third panel in series would reach 73.8 V, so 400 W is the most these panels can deliver to one Elite 200 V2 input.
  • Idle alone empties the battery in: 1,910 Wh ÷ 352 Wh = about 5.4 sunless days.
  • 12 V kit ceiling: 12.8 V × 100 A = 1,280 W at the battery. A 2,000 W inverter running at full rating needs 2,000 ÷ 12.8 = about 156 A, more than a 100 A battery allows.

The idle draw that sizes a shed kit

A power station's inverter uses power whenever AC is switched on, even with nothing plugged in. Hobotech measured the Elite 200 V2 using 88 Wh in 6 hours with AC on and no load: 88 ÷ 6 = 14.7 W, about 352 Wh per 24 hours. With only the DC outputs on, it used 72 Wh in 12 hours: 6 W, about 144 Wh a day. Hobotech called 15 W at idle exceptional for a 2,600 W inverter. In a shed you visit on weekends, though, that draw runs all week.

In a northern winter one 200 W panel brings in about 375 Wh a day, and 352 Wh of that (94%) goes to idle. Two panels bring in 750 Wh and leave 398 Wh for lights and tools. The free fix is a setting. StorageReview notes that ECO mode can be turned on for the AC or DC outputs to cut idle draw, so switch AC off or set ECO when you leave. If you also run 12 V lights from DC, the two panels leave 750 − 144 = 606 Wh a day.
