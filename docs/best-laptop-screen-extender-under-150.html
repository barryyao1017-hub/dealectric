What the differences mean

Two screens against one. A 14-inch 16:9 screen is 12.20 × 6.86 in, or 83.75 sq in; two of them add 167.5 sq in. A 15.6-inch 16:9 screen is 13.60 × 7.65 in, or 104.0 sq in (ASUS states 344.16 × 193.59 mm for its 15.6-inch panel, which is 103.3 sq in). 167.5 ÷ 104.0 = 1.61, so the two-screen set adds 1.61 times the area of one 15.6-inch monitor.

Same pixels on every row. Each screen here is 1920×1080, a 2,203-pixel diagonal. 2,203 ÷ 14 = 157 pixels per inch; 2,203 ÷ 15.6 = 141. A 15.6-inch screen shows the same amount of content as a 14-inch one, drawn larger. Only a second screen adds workspace: 2 × 2.074 = 4.15 million pixels against 2.07 million.

Battery. These screens draw their power from the laptop. As a guide to the class, not a figure for any row: ASUS rates its 15.6-inch 1080p ZenScreen MB169CK at 7.64 W, and Apple lists a 52.6 Wh battery in the M2 MacBook Air. 7.64 ÷ 52.6 = 14.5% of that battery per hour for one screen of that kind.

How many of the added screens your laptop will light

A driver-free screen is driven the way any monitor is: the laptop sends it native video, over a USB-C port that carries DisplayPort Alt Mode or Thunderbolt, or over HDMI. Each added screen then counts as one external display, and the laptop's own limit applies.

  • M1 MacBook Air: Apple lists "One external display with up to 6K resolution at 60Hz" alongside the built-in display.
  • M2 MacBook Air: Apple lists one external display, in the same words.
  • M3 MacBook Air (13-inch): one external display with the lid open. Apple's wording for a second is "Close the MacBook Air lid to use a second external display", which gives up the MacBook's own screen.

Simon Jary of Macworld puts it in one line in his review of a dual extender: "the plain M1/M2 MacBooks support just one extra screen". Set that against the table. FOPO lists the S1 as driver-free with two screens, so on those MacBook Airs one of its two screens lights and the other stays dark. That is our reading of Apple's figure and FOPO's label, not a statement FOPO makes about Macs. The three single monitors add one display and stay inside the limit.

On a Windows laptop the check is the same, counted per screen: one video-capable output for each added screen. Mobile Pixels' footnote for the Fold reads "For device has USB-C port with DP ALT mode", and UPERFECT names a Thunderbolt 3 laptop for its one-cable use. For how a pair wired this way behaves, the one lab test of a FOPO extender we read is Allen Ngo's at Notebookcheck (2022), of an older 12-inch pair that is a different model from the S1: on a laptop with two video-capable USB-C ports it takes two USB-C to USB-C cables, "one cable for each monitor to the laptop", and Ngo writes that the two screens "should appear as two ordinary external monitors to Windows".

Brightness figures for these screens are the makers' claims: 250 cd/m² for the S1 (FOPO) and 300 nits for the Fold (Mobile Pixels). No named reviewer we read has measured either. Where a portable monitor of this class has been measured, the claim ran high: Sasha Muller at Expert Reviews measured the ASUS ZenScreen MB169CK at 205 cd/m² against ASUS's 250 cd/m², which is 82% of the claim, with 62% sRGB coverage. Not recommended

A two-screen, driver-free extender for an M1 or M2 MacBook Air, or an M3 Air you use with the lid open. Apple's limit of one external display means you would pay for two 14-inch screens and use one, 83.75 sq in, when a single 15.6-inch monitor gives 104.0 sq in. On those laptops buy one screen.
