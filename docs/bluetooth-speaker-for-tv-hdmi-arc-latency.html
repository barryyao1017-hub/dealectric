What the differences mean

  • Sony HT-S100F delay in film frames: one frame at 24 fps lasts 1000 ÷ 24 = 41.7 ms. HDMI ARC: 62 ÷ 41.7 = 1.5 frames. Optical: 70 ÷ 41.7 = 1.7 frames.
  • Margin under the lip-sync limit: ITU-R BT.1359 puts the detectability limit at 125 ms of audio lag. Over ARC that leaves 125 − 62 = 63 ms. The stricter ATSC IS-191 broadcast target is 45 ms of lag (1.1 frames), and the bar's 62 ms goes over it by 17 ms. Most viewers will still see speech in sync, but the bar doesn't meet the broadcast-grade target.
  • Bluetooth from a phone, for comparison: GadgetReview measured the original SoundLink Flex (2021) at 109 ms from Android and 236 ms from iOS. That is 109 ÷ 62 = 1.8× and 236 ÷ 62 = 3.8× the soundbar's ARC delay, and the iOS figure is 236 − 125 = 111 ms past the ITU limit. These were phone tests on the first-generation model, so they show the direction of the gap, not a TV figure for the 2nd Gen.
  • Battery for TV use: SoundGuys measured the Bose Flex (2nd Gen) at 7 h 3 min. 7.05 ÷ 3 = 2.35, so you get 2 full three-hour evenings per charge. Bose's own 12 h claim at 50% volume works out to 12 ÷ 3 = 4 evenings.
  • Wattage on the box: Sony's US manuals rate both the HT-S100F and the larger HT-SD40 at 20 W per channel minimum RMS (FTC, no more than 1% THD). The headline totals add up single-frequency reference figures: 4 × 30 W = 120 W for the S100F, and 100 + 100 + 130 W = 330 W for the SD40. 130 of the SD40's 330 W (39%) is its subwoofer, so the larger bar adds bass, not louder dialogue.

Our pick for most TVs: the Sony HT-S100F, connected to the TV by HDMI ARC.

Why the cable wins: delay, sync controls and inputs

Bluetooth encodes the TV's audio, sends it over the air and buffers it at the speaker, and every step adds delay. The Sony HT-S100F's Bluetooth receiver supports only the SBC codec (Sony manual), so a TV's Bluetooth signal reaches it without any low-latency codec. The bar's HDMI ARC and optical inputs avoid the radio link altogether. That is where GadgetReview's 62 ms and 70 ms come from.

A soundbar's lip-sync setting won't rescue a Bluetooth link. Samsung's manual for the B-series HW-B650 (a sibling of the HW-B630F) says its AUDIO SYNC control only delays the audio, by 0–300 ms, and is 'Not available in "USB" or "BT" mode'. Over Bluetooth the sound is already behind the picture, and adding delay would push it further behind. If you go cable-free, the only correction left is whatever your TV's own audio-delay setting offers.

Check the inputs before you buy a portable speaker for TV use. The Bose SoundLink Flex (2nd Gen) has no AUX input (SoundGuys), and Marshall lists no wired audio input for the Emberton III. Both work with a TV only if the TV can send audio over Bluetooth. If yours can't, you would need a separate transmitter such as the buy on Amazon: https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0838YPSZT?tag=dealectric-20, which takes optical, coaxial or 3.5 mm audio from the TV. Not recommended

Bose SoundLink Flex (2nd Gen) as your main TV speaker. It's a fine music speaker, but it has no wired input, so it depends entirely on the TV's Bluetooth output. SoundGuys measured 7 h 3 min per charge, so it needs charging every 2 evenings of three-hour viewing. GadgetReview's phone tests on the earlier model measured 109–236 ms of Bluetooth delay, up to 3.8 times the HT-S100F's ARC figure.
