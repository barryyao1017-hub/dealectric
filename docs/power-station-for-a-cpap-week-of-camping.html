DC converter or AC adapter

ResMed's AirSense 11 DC/DC converter (part 39231) takes 12 V or 24 V in and feeds the machine's 24 V input with the humidifier and ClimateLine tube working, shutting off if the supply sags; it draws up to about 8 A from a 12 V car port at the machine's 73 W peak, inside every station's 10 A / 120 W port. One forum measurement put an AC adapter plus inverter at 64–70 % efficient and a DC converter at 72–78 %, a modest gap; the bigger saving is the inverter's standby draw, measured at 15 W on the AC180 and 19 W on the Elite 30 V2, which over an 8-hour night is 120–150 Wh, more than the dry machine itself. The AirSense 11 has no USB-C input. Use ResMed's converter, not a generic one: sellers warn a non-ResMed converter can damage the machine and void its warranty.

ECO mode and the overnight cut-off

BLUETTI's ECO setting switches outputs off after hours of low load (thresholds of roughly 10–50 W on other models), and owners report CPAPs stopping in the night. Turn ECO off before bed; with it off the station idles down slowly even with nothing plugged in, which is the price of a reliable night.

Refilling during the week

A 100 W panel returns about 300–375 Wh on a clear day (less than 2 peak-sun hours in a November valley cuts that by half or more), a 200 W panel 600–750 Wh, roughly a humidifier-on night. Mind the input windows: the Elite 30 V2 accepts 12–28 V and the T320 5–24 V, which rules out most 100 W-plus folding panels (BLUETTI's own 100 W kit, $489.99, fits the Elite 30 V2). Car charging adds about 100 Wh an hour on the AC180 (a full charge in 10–13 hours) and 96 W on the Elite 200 V2. For air travel the FAA allows 100 Wh batteries freely and 101–160 Wh with airline approval; every station here is over 160 Wh and stays home.
