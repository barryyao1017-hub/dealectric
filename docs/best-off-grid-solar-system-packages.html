Peak-sun hours to refill: the figure kit listings leave out

A kit's panels have to refill its battery between one night and the next. Battery watt-hours divided by panel watts gives the hours of full sun (peak-sun hours) the kit needs to go from empty to full. That figure assumes no losses and no daytime load. Real charging takes longer, because heat, wiring and charging losses cut panel output, and anything running during the day uses part of the harvest. A kit whose refill time is longer than the sun hours you actually get in winter can't recover from one cloudy day.

The makers' own sun-hour bases show the gap. SunGoldPower rates the SGR-8K10E at about 14.4 kWh a day from 4 hours of full sun (3,600 W × 4 h), which is more than its 10.24 kWh battery. On the same 4-hour basis the SGM-6510M makes 2,220 W × 4 h = 8.9 kWh, which is less than its own 10.24 kWh battery. Renogy bases its kits on 5–6 peak-sun hours, and at that rate the 400W kit makes 2.0–2.4 kWh a day against a 3.6 kWh battery, so a full refill takes 1.5–1.8 days.

Room to add panels matters as well. The SGR-8K10E's inverter accepts up to 11,000 W of PV on two MPPT inputs, about three times the 3,600 W it ships with, so the refill time can be cut later. The 3,500 W all-in-one inverter in the ECO-WORTHY 1560W kit lists 4,400 W maximum PV input and 145 VDC maximum open-circuit voltage on its own product page, so that kit has much less room to grow.
