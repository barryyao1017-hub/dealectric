What the differences mean

  • Usable energy = rated capacity × measured AC efficiency. Elite 200 V2: 2,073.6 × 0.94 = 1,949 Wh. C2000 Gen 2: 2,048 × 0.89 = 1,823 Wh. DELTA 3 Ultra: 3,072 × 0.82 = 2,519 Wh. DELTA 3 Max: 2,048 × 0.79 = 1,618 Wh. Elite 300: 2,650 Wh, measured directly by Notebookcheck.
  • 5,000 BTU, compressor running nonstop at StationHours' 450 W: 1,949 ÷ 450 = 4.3 h on the Elite 200 V2, against 1,618 ÷ 450 = 3.6 h on the DELTA 3 Max. That is about 0.7 h more from a battery of almost the same size.
  • 8,000 BTU at BuyerSheet's 420 W average (700 W running, compressor on about 60% of the time): 2,650 ÷ 420 = 6.3 h on the Elite 300 and 1,949 ÷ 420 = 4.6 h on the Elite 200 V2.
  • Starting the compressor: a 5,000 BTU unit spikes to about 1,300 W (StationHours), which is under every station's continuous rating. An 8,000 BTU unit needs about 2,100 W to start (BuyerSheet). That leaves 2,600 − 2,100 = 500 W of margin on the Elite 200 V2, 2,400 − 2,100 = 300 W on the 2,400 W units, and 3,600 − 2,100 = 1,500 W on the DELTA 3 Ultra.
  • Cost per usable Wh at BLUETTI's store today: Elite 200 V2 at $899 ÷ 1,949 Wh = $0.46. Elite 300 at $1,199 ÷ 2,650 Wh = $0.45.

Measured efficiency and idle draw: where the air conditioner's hours go

A power station loses energy turning battery DC into 120 V AC, and that loss differs by model. The Solar Lab measured 94% on the Elite 200 V2, about 89% on the C2000 Gen 2, about 82% on the DELTA 3 Ultra and 79% on the DELTA 3 Max. APH Networks checked the Elite 200 V2 separately and drained 2,028 Wh at a steady 987 W, which is 97.8% of the rated 2,073.6 Wh.

Idle draw matters because a window AC's compressor switches off once the room reaches the set temperature, but the inverter keeps drawing power. Over an 8-hour night, 9.5 W × 8 = 76 Wh on the Elite 200 V2, against 27 W × 8 = 216 Wh on the DELTA 3 Max. The 140 Wh difference is about 19 more minutes of compressor time at 450 W (140 ÷ 450 = 0.31 h).

Efficiency also changes with load. Notebookcheck measured the C2000 Gen 2 at 79% at 200 W and 90% at 1,800 W, and the Elite 300 at just under 88% at 200 W and just over 92% at 1,800 W. Both were EU 230 V models. A window AC draws roughly 450 to 700 W, between those test loads, so treat the hours above as a guide. None of these reviewers ran a real window AC on these stations.

The boost figures do not help a compressor. BLUETTI limits the Elite 200 V2's 3,900 W Power Lifting to 'pure resistive loads'. StorageReview cites the Elite 300 manual: output 'remains at 2,400W' in Power Lifting. StorageReview's Dylan Bryan adds that the mode is 'not a startup allowance for motors or compressors'. APH Networks ran two hair dryers on the Elite 200 V2: the draw spiked to 3,140 W and the unit shut down after about 5 seconds. The Solar Lab held the same unit at 3,000 W for over 2 minutes. Anker's 4,000 W peak comes with no stated duration, though The Solar Lab saw up to 6,000 W in short bursts. Size the compressor start against the continuous rating.

Not recommended

EcoFlow DELTA 3 Max for overnight cooling. It is in the same 2,048 Wh class as the C2000 Gen 2, but The Solar Lab measured the lowest efficiency (79%) and highest idle draw (about 27 W) of the stations here. That gives 3.6 hours on a 5,000 BTU unit, against 4.3 hours from the Elite 200 V2. Its 68-minute wall recharge (The Solar Lab) suits short runs with an outlet nearby better than a night on battery.
