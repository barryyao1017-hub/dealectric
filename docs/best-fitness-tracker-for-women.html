Which trackers read your body for cycle tracking

A cycle prediction only reflects your body if the tracker measures something your body does. Here that means skin temperature. The Apple Watch SE 3 samples wrist temperature every five seconds while you sleep. After about five nights it uses those readings to estimate ovulation after the fact and to adjust period predictions. Apple notes that a loose fit can affect the readings. Oura's Ring 5 also measures temperature trends, and its Period Prediction looks for a drop in body temperature.

The other two don't measure it. the5krunner's spec sheet for the Garmin Lily 2 Active lists 'Skin temperature: No'. Fitbit's help page says the fertile window 'automatically adjusts based on the cycle data you enter', and it needs at least 3 completed cycles before it shows trends. Both are fine period diaries. Neither can spot ovulation from your body.

Birth control is a separate question. Apple says its Cycle Tracking app should not be used for birth control. Natural Cycles is an FDA-cleared contraceptive app (2018): 93% effective with typical use and 98% with perfect use. It accepts temperature only from devices it has validated, and it says 'any other wearables are not intended to be used with Natural Cycles'. Its list includes the Apple Watch SE3, Series 8 and later, and Ultra models; Oura Ring 2 through Ring 5; and Garmin models that have a temperature sensor (Venu 3/3S/4/4S/X1, Forerunner 570/970, fēnix 8 and others). The Lily 2 Active and vívoactive 6 are not on it. No Fitbit is on it either, and Natural Cycles says not to type in Fitbit temperature data by hand. Natural Cycles is its own subscription. With Oura on Gen 3 or later, you also need an Oura membership.

Not recommended

An older Apple Watch SE (1st or 2nd generation) bought for cycle tracking. Natural Cycles states that these 'do not have temperature sensors', so they can only log your period, the same as a basic band. If temperature-based cycle data is the reason you're buying, get the SE 3.
