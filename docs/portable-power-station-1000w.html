What the differences mean

“1000 watt” can mean 1,000 W of output (how much the outlets can run at once) or 1,000 Wh of battery (how long it lasts). Every station here meets both: at least 1,500 W of output and about 1 kWh of battery. Dealectric does not test units. The measured figures are OutdoorGearLab's, and the figures below are arithmetic on them.

  • Usable energy per pound: Jackery 910 ÷ 24.4 = 37.3 Wh/lb; Elite 100 V2 890 ÷ 25.0 = 35.6; Anker 890 ÷ 25.2 = 35.3; DELTA 3 870 ÷ 28.1 = 31.0; DJI 880 ÷ 31.5 = 27.9.
  • Hours at a 100 W load: Jackery 910 ÷ 100 = 9.1 h; Anker and Elite 890 ÷ 100 = 8.9 h; DJI 8.8 h; DELTA 3 8.7 h.
  • Wall refill from empty at the measured rate: DELTA 3 1,024 ÷ 1,229 = 0.83 h; Anker 1,024 ÷ 1,097 = 0.93 h; DJI 1,024 ÷ 1,041 = 0.98 h; Jackery 1,070 ÷ 563 = 1.9 h; Elite 1,024 ÷ 512 = 2.0 h.
  • Best-case solar refill at rated input, before losses: Elite 1,024 ÷ 1,000 W = 1.0 h; DJI 1,024 ÷ 800 = 1.3 h; Anker 1,024 ÷ 600 = 1.7 h; DELTA 3 1,024 ÷ 500 = 2.0 h; Jackery 1,070 ÷ 400 = 2.7 h.
  • Cost per usable Wh (prices read 2026-11-15): DJI $399.98 (Amazon) ÷ 880 Wh = $0.45; Elite 100 V2 $499 (BLUETTI store) ÷ 890 Wh = $0.56; DELTA 3 $579 (EcoFlow store) ÷ 870 Wh = $0.67.

On this page's figures, the Elite 100 V2 wins for solar charging, with the highest rated input of the five (1,000 W), but it is not the cheapest per usable Wh: its $0.56 beats the DELTA 3's $0.67 but loses to the DJI's $0.45 on Amazon.

Measured usable energy, scale weight and refill speed

A 1 kWh station never delivers its full rating at the outlet: the inverter and the battery's reserve take a share. OutdoorGearLab's discharge tests got 85–87% back from all five, so the five are only 40 Wh apart in usable energy (870–910 Wh). Weight and refill speed separate them more. On OutdoorGearLab's scale the Jackery weighed 24.4 lb against Jackery's claim of about 10.8 kg (23.8 lb), and the Anker 25.2 lb against Anker's 24.9 lb.

Refill claims need a closer look. Anker's 49-minute full charge only applies with UltraFast turned on in the Anker app. OutdoorGearLab measured 56 minutes in normal charging. BLUETTI's 45-minute figure for the Elite 100 V2 is to 80%, not full. OutdoorGearLab measured 512 Wh/hr and lists slow charging as a con, and The Solar Lab saw it slow to about 500 W below about 20% charge, for a full charge in about 1 h 39 min. Jackery's FAQ gives the Explorer 1000 v2 about 1.7 h on AC and lists its emergency charging mode as not supported. Jackery also rates its cycle life to ≥70% capacity, while Anker and BLUETTI rate theirs to ≥80%.

Noise and solar on the Elite 100 V2. OutdoorGearLab wrote that the Elite's fan “stayed remarkably quiet.” Notebookcheck (Christian Hintze) notes that BLUETTI's under-30 dB claim applies only in Silent mode, which caps power at 600 W. Notebookcheck also calls the 1,000 W solar input “difficult in practice”: a single BLUETTI PV350 panel's 46.5 V makes series wiring impractical under the 60 V limit, and two panels would still deliver only about 700 W. The Solar Lab calculated about 16 W of idle draw with the inverter on. That is 16 × 24 = 384 Wh a day, 43% of the 890 Wh usable, so run small overnight loads from the DC ports. OutdoorGearLab scored the Elite 65, the lowest of these five, and notes that no car or solar cable comes in the box. It suits buyers who charge mainly from solar and keep loads under 600 W when they want it quiet: $449.99 on bluettipower.com, $427.50 through this link: https://dealectric.com/go/bluetti-elite-100-v2. The Anker's fan, by contrast, “gets frustratingly loud” near maximum load (OutdoorGearLab), and Notebookcheck measured about 45 dB at 1 m. Anker's solar tops out at 600 W, and OutdoorGearLab notes that it no longer takes an expansion battery. For carrying, choose the Jackery: buy on Amazon: https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0D7PPG25F?tag=dealectric-20.

Not recommended

BLUETTI Elite 100 Mini for a 1,000 W need. It is small (23.6 lb, 1,004.8 Wh), but BLUETTI's own page says “Total output not to exceed 700W.” Its 1,400 W Power Lifting Mode, per BLUETTI's launch copy, covers only “compatible resistive appliances,” so it won't run a 1,000 W appliance that has a motor or compressor.
