What the differences mean

  • Heat stored in the water: 283 L × 4,186 J per litre per °C × 5 °C ÷ 3,600 = 1,645 Wh between 77 °F and 68 °F.
  • Wrapped tank, 59 °F room: 1,645 Wh × 1.386 ÷ 44 h = 52 W of heat loss. A 300 W heater runs 17% of the time, about 1,250 Wh a day.
  • Glass lid only, 59 °F room: 1,645 × 1.386 ÷ 12.4 h = 184 W, a 61% duty cycle, about 4,400 Wh a day.
  • Open top: AquariumToolbox puts the loss at 323 W. That is more than a 300 W heater delivers, so the water cools no matter how big the battery is.
  • Elite 300, wrapped, 59 °F: 2,650 Wh ÷ (52 + 17.5 + 19) W = 29.9 h, about 6 hours to spare.
  • Elite 200 V2, same tank: 1,910 ÷ (52 + 17.5 + 10) = 24.0 h, with nothing to spare.
  • DELTA 2 Max, same tank: 1,800 ÷ (52 + 17.5 + 13.6) = 21.7 h.
  • Inverter idle: 19 W × 24 h = 456 Wh a day on the Elite 300. That is more than a third of the wrapped tank's daily heater energy.

The heater's duty cycle comes from the tank's heat loss

A heater's thermostat switches on only to replace heat the tank loses through the glass and the water surface. Its duty cycle is therefore the tank's heat loss divided by the heater's rating, and you can work it out instead of guessing. AquariumToolbox's outage calculator gives the time a 75-gallon tank at 77 °F takes to cool to 68 °F in a house at 59 °F: 6.8 h with an open top, 12.4 h with a glass lid and 44 h with blankets over the top and sides. Cooling from 77 °F to 68 °F halves the 18 °F gap to the room. Under Newton's law of cooling, the loss at the full gap is the stored 1,645 Wh × 2 × ln 2 ÷ the cooling time, which is the 1.386 factor above. As a check, the open-tank time gives 335 W, within 4% of the calculator's own 323 W.

Wrapping the tank cuts the heater's load from 184 W to 52 W, about 3.5 times less. That does more than a second battery would: a lidded tank in a 59 °F room needs (184 + 17.5 + 19) W × 24 h ≈ 5.3 kWh, about double what any station here delivers. Room temperature matters next. In a 68 °F room the gap to the water halves, so the loss roughly halves too. That gives 26 W wrapped and 92 W lidded; this halving is our own estimate, not a calculator figure.

Each runtime starts from the station's measured AC output from a named tester. Notebookcheck measured the Elite 300 at just over 200 W, the closest load to an aquarium's. The measured idle draw is then added on top, so the runtimes err on the short side. The filter and air pump are AquariumToolbox's 15 W and 2.5 W examples. With a 23 W canister and a 4 W air pump, the Elite 300's wrapped runtime drops to 2,650 ÷ (52 + 23 + 4 + 19) = 27.0 h.

Turn ECO off before the outage. BLUETTI's Elite 300 manual says "AC-ECO and DC-ECO modes are enabled by default". In that mode the station "shuts off the AC or DC output after a period of low or no load", and the manual tells owners to disable ECO for critical appliances. Pressing the AC power button toggles both modes. The manual also says AC-ECO "is not available when charging with AC power", so a test while the station is plugged into the wall won't show the cut-off. Unplug the station and run the tank on battery for an evening to check. The AC180's manual lets you set the AC threshold anywhere from 10 to 30 W, for 1 to 4 hours, and a 17.5 W filter-and-pump load falls inside that range. The Technology Man measured the Elite 300's switch to battery at 8.9 ms; BLUETTI rates it under 10 ms.

Not recommended

BLUETTI AC180 for a heated tank. Even with the tank wrapped in a 59 °F room it lasts 1,030 ÷ (52 + 17.5 + 20) = 11.5 h. planetfish.org recorded 3 h 6 min on a 340 W reef load. It earns its place only as a filter-and-air-pump backup with the heater unplugged, where it lasts about 27 hours with ECO off.
