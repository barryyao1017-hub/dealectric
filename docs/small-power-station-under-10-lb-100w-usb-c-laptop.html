How many laptop charges, and why it's an estimate

Battery sizes: 13-inch MacBook Air M4 53.8 Wh, 15-inch about 66.5 Wh, 14-inch MacBook Pro 72.4 Wh, 16-inch about 100 Wh. The only instrumented USB-C test we found is techtest.org's on Anker's C300X DC, which delivered 270–273 Wh of its 288 Wh at 9, 20 and 28 V (about 95 %); charging a laptop also loses some energy inside the laptop. So a 288 Wh unit gives roughly 4–5 charges of a 13-inch Air, 3–3.5 of a 14-inch Pro and 2–2.5 of a 16-inch, before those losses; MacRumors counted 3.5 MacBook Air charges on the Elite 30 V2. StorageReview measured 210 Wh from the RIVER 3 on AC, 86 % of its rating, which is lower than USB-C delivery would be because of the inverter.

Ports decide the speed

A 14- or 16-inch MacBook Pro charges fastest at 96–140 W; a 100 W port charges it at near full speed, a 45 W port (Tenergy T320) slowly, and a gaming laptop's 180–240 W brick can't be replaced by any USB-C port here, only the AC outlet. Two USB-C ports matter when two people share a weekend: the Elite 30 V2 and the DC-only Anker and Jackery units have them, the RIVER 3 and Jackery 240 v2 have one at 100 W.

Under 10 lb, and what's just over

The EcoFlow RIVER 3 Plus (10.4 lb), Goal Zero Yeti 300 (under 14 lb), DJI Power 500 (about 16 lb) and our ALLPOWERS R600 (12.3 lb) are over the line. Weights are the makers' figures. MacRumors also measured the Elite 30 V2 losing about 10 % in four hours with its DC output on and nothing plugged in, so switch unused outputs off.
