Can you host a WordPress site on a NAS at home instead of paying for hosting? (2026)

Updated 2026-11-08

Short answer. You can host a WordPress site on a NAS at home, and for a public site that matters you shouldn't: shared hosting renews at about $10–11 a month (Bluehost, Hostinger, DreamHost; $2–4 a month on a 1–4-year prepay) and a $4–6 VPS does the same, with uptime, upload bandwidth and patching that a box under your desk cannot match. For a private site, a staging copy, a learning project or a low-traffic hobby blog, buy the UGREEN NASync DXP2800 ($389.99; buy: https://dealectric.com/go/ugreen-nasync-dxp2800), $389.99 diskless on 2026-11-03, run the WordPress + MariaDB + Redis stack in its Docker app, and publish it through a Cloudflare Tunnel rather than an open port.

What to know: Xfinity's residential acceptable-use policy prohibits running servers that provide network content from the premises and names web hosting as an example, so check your own ISP's terms (Spectrum and AT&T wording not verified); Starlink and 5G home internet sit behind carrier-grade NAT, which blocks inbound ports entirely; a Cloudflare Tunnel makes only outbound connections, needs no port forwarding and sidesteps both, but Cloudflare's CDN terms restrict serving video and "a disproportionate percentage of pictures" through the free tier, so an image-heavy gallery is a grey area. The DXP2800 is more than enough CPU for a small site (the DXP4800 Plus adds nothing here); UGREEN publishes no WordPress guide, so you follow a third-party Portainer stack with Nginx Proxy Manager for HTTPS, while Synology's DS225+ (about $275–340) has a one-click WordPress package in Web Station and QNAP a QTS tutorial. Running cost is about $1 a month of electricity plus a $10–15 domain; the costs that matter are your time and the risk that a power cut, an ISP outage or a firmware reboot takes the site down while you sleep.

Why a home NAS is a poor public host

  • Terms of service: Xfinity's residential AUP bars "dedicated, stand-alone equipment or servers from the Premises that provide network content" and lists web hosting among them, and limits the service to personal, non-commercial use; how strictly it is enforced is undocumented. Other ISPs' wording was not verified; read yours.
  • Reachability: Starlink and T-Mobile 5G home internet use carrier-grade NAT, so port forwarding does not work; a dynamic IP on cable needs DDNS; a Cloudflare Tunnel (cloudflared dialling out to Cloudflare, your hostname proxied back) solves both without an open port.
  • Bandwidth and uptime: every visitor rides your home upload link, and the site is down for every power cut, ISP outage and firmware reboot; hosts sell 99.9 %-plus uptime with credits.
  • Security: WordPress is the most attacked CMS; on a NAS the WordPress container sits next to your family's files. Keep it in its own container with no share mounts, keep phpMyAdmin off the proxy, enable auto-updates and a firewall plugin, and treat the NAS admin password as the crown jewel.

When it honestly wins

A documentation or family site on the LAN; a staging copy of a hosted site you can break; learning Docker, reverse proxies and certificates; a blog with a few visitors a day behind a tunnel, where the NAS was bought for storage anyway and the site is free. Cloudflare's free CDN terms (no video, no disproportionate share of images or large files without its paid services) are the one thing to read before putting a photo-heavy site through the tunnel.

The stack on UGOS

UGREEN's Docker app takes Compose projects (UGOS Pro 1.10+); the published UGREEN recipe is a Portainer stack with wordpress, mariadb, redis and phpMyAdmin under /volume1/docker/wordpress with the NAS user's UID and GID, WordPress on a high port, Nginx Proxy Manager in front with a Let's Encrypt certificate, HSTS and HTTP/2, and HTTPS enabled on the NAS itself. UGOS has no documented native Let's Encrypt or reverse proxy, which is why the proxy runs in Docker; Synology's Web Station bundles both.
