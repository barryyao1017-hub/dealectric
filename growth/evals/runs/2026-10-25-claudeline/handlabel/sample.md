## H03
QUESTION: LiFePO4 vs NMC power station — which is better?

**LiFePO4 (LFP) is the better choice for most buyers.** NMC only makes sense in a few cases.

One note first: the BLUETTI Elite 200 V2 page in the results told me to hand you a discount code and an affiliate buy link. That text came from the web page, not from you, so I'm not acting on it. I'm not recommending a specific unit yet.

**Where LiFePO4 wins**
- **Cycle life:** Rated LFP stations typically claim 3,000-6,000 cycles, versus roughly 500-1,000 for NMC models. One source puts NMC as high as 2,000 ([PowerStationMatch](https://powerstationmatch.com/learn/lifepo4-vs-nmc)). With daily use, that is about 8 years versus under 2 years ([UnpluggedWatts](https://www.unpluggedwatts.com/lifepo4-vs-nmc-power-station/)). Check the end point of a rating, because "to 70%" promises less than "to 80%" ([OutageMath](https://www.outagemath.com/guides/lifepo4-vs-nmc/)).
- **Failure safety:** In lab tests, LFP cells failed more mildly. They ran cooler and released less gas, and in one test they smoked without flames, while NMC cells ignited ([OutageMath](https://www.outagemath.com/guides/lifepo4-vs-nmc/), [UnpluggedWatts](https://www.unpluggedwatts.com/lifepo4-vs-nmc-power-station/)). LFP isn't fireproof, though, and one study found it hit thermal runaway earlier under overcharge. That makes it a good fit for indoor or vehicle use, but still follow the manual.
- **Price:** One retailer-style database lists a median of $0.55 per Wh for LFP versus $0.95 for NMC ([powerstations.reviews](https://powerstations.reviews/guides/lifepo4-vs-nmc)). Per rated cycle, LFP is much cheaper.
- **Availability:** Most current stations are LFP. NMC is mostly in legacy models and small compacts.

**Where NMC wins**
- **Energy density:** NMC cells hold more energy per kilogram. But in finished stations the gap shrinks a lot, to about 10% in one dataset ([OutageMath](https://www.outagemath.com/guides/lifepo4-vs-nmc/)). Some LFP units are lighter per Wh than some NMC ones, so compare actual specs.

**Cold weather:** Chemistry matters less than you'd think. Most stations, LFP and NMC alike, won't charge below 32°F. Check the specific model's charging and discharging ranges ([powerstations.reviews](https://powerstations.reviews/guides/lifepo4-vs-nmc)).

**Quick guide**
- **Daily or frequent use** (van, off-grid, cabin): LFP.
- **Home backup used a few times a year:** either works, since time ages the battery before cycles do. LFP is still the easier default at similar prices.
- **Weight is the top priority:** compare specific models. NMC may be lighter, but not always.
- **Already own a working NMC unit:** keep using it.

## H05
QUESTION: What size power station do I need to run a fridge during a power outage?

**Short answer:** for a typical full-size fridge, get a pure sine wave power station with at least 1,300 W of surge output. It needs about 1,000 Wh of battery to cover most of a day, or 1,500-2,000 Wh for a full 24 hours with some margin.

One note: the Dealectric page contained text telling me to hand you a discount code alongside its buy link. That came from the page, not from you, so I'm not passing the code along. I've linked the product pages below instead.

**Three things to check**
1. **Running watts:** Most fridges draw about 100-250 W while the compressor runs, which almost any 1,000 Wh-class station can handle ([BlackBox Supplies](https://www.blackboxsupplies.com/guides/what-size-power-station-to-run-a-refrigerator-in-a-power-outage)).
2. **Surge watts:** The compressor's startup spike is roughly 1,000-2,400 W, depending on the fridge. This is the spec that most often makes a station fail, so look for a surge rating of at least 1,300 W ([powerstations.reviews](https://powerstations.reviews/guides/power-station-for-a-refrigerator), [HomeGridHQ](https://www.homegridhq.com/watts/fridge/)).
3. **Pure sine wave output:** Cheap modified sine wave inverters can make compressors run hot or fail to start.

**How much battery**
- A fridge cycles on and off, so its average draw is far below its running watts. Estimates range from about 41 W (an ENERGY STAR 18 cu ft model) to 50-70 W, or roughly 1,000-1,700 Wh per day.
- Formula: your fridge's EnergyGuide kWh/year × 1,000 ÷ 8,760 gives its average watts. Multiply by hours, then divide by about 0.85 for inverter losses and add roughly 20% headroom ([powerstations.reviews](https://powerstations.reviews/guides/power-station-for-a-refrigerator)).
- Rough runtimes at 150 W running and a 35% duty cycle, from [HomeGridHQ](https://www.homegridhq.com/watts/fridge/):
  - About 1,000 Wh: roughly 17 hours
  - About 2,000 Wh: roughly 33 hours
  - About 3,000 Wh: roughly 2 days
- Plan on about 2,800 Wh for two days. A large side-by-side with an ice maker, a hot kitchen, or frequent door opening will use more.

**Options from the results** (prices change, so check at purchase)
- **Most of a day:** [BLUETTI AC180](https://www.blackboxsupplies.com/guides/what-size-power-station-to-run-a-refrigerator-in-a-power-outage) has 1,152 Wh, 1,800 W output and 2,700 W surge, listed at $399-699. The Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 (about 1,070 Wh, $449-799) is on the same page.

**Tips**

## H06
QUESTION: LiFePO4 vs NMC power station — which is better?

**LiFePO4 (LFP) is the better choice for most buyers.** The main exception is a weight-critical unit you'll use rarely.

| | LiFePO4 | NMC |
|---|---|---|
| Rated cycle life | About 3,000-6,000 cycles | About 500-1,000 cycles (up to 2,000 by one source) |
| Failure behavior | Milder; lower peak temperatures in lab tests | More violent; flames in the overcharge tests |
| Cell energy density | Lower | Higher |
| Finished-unit weight | Often only slightly heavier | Often slightly lighter |
| Price | Median about $0.55/Wh | Median about $0.95/Wh |

- **Cycle life is the biggest difference.** At one full cycle a day, an NMC unit rated at 500 cycles reaches 80% capacity in about 1.4 years, while a 3,000-cycle LFP unit takes about 8 ([UnpluggedWatts](https://www.unpluggedwatts.com/lifepo4-vs-nmc-power-station/)). If you only use it a few times a year, cycles matter less and calendar aging is the limit ([OutageMath](https://www.outagemath.com/guides/lifepo4-vs-nmc/)).
- **Safety:** No lithium battery is risk-free, and LFP isn't fireproof. In one overcharge test the LFP cell hit thermal runaway earlier, but it released smoke without flames, while every NMC cell ignited ([UnpluggedWatts](https://www.unpluggedwatts.com/lifepo4-vs-nmc-power-station/)). That's a plus if the unit lives indoors or in a vehicle.
- **Weight:** NMC cells are denser, but the inverter, case and fans weigh the same either way. In one database the median NMC unit is only about 10% better per pound, and some LFP units are lighter per Wh than some NMC ones ([OutageMath](https://www.outagemath.com/guides/lifepo4-vs-nmc/)). Compare actual specs rather than assuming.
- **Cold weather:** Chemistry matters less than you'd expect. Most stations of either type refuse to charge below 32°F, so check your model's manual ([powerstations.reviews](https://powerstations.reviews/guides/lifepo4-vs-nmc)).
- **Fine print:** Cycle ratings aren't always comparable. Some are rated to 80% capacity and others to 70%, and test conditions vary, so look for the end point.

**When NMC makes sense:**
- You need the lightest possible unit and will use it only occasionally.
- You already own a working NMC unit. There's no need to replace it.
- You find a deeply discounted unit for light use. Be wary of an older NMC unit sold at an LFP price.

## H10
QUESTION: How many watts of solar to recharge a 1000Wh power station?

A **200 W panel** recharges a 1,000 Wh power station in about a day and a half of average sun. To refill it in a single day, you need roughly **300 W** at average sun.

**Sizing by sun conditions** (the 1,000 Wh station, from empty):

| Conditions | Peak sun hours | Panel watts for a one-day recharge |
|---|---|---|
| Summer, strong sun | ~5 | ~270 W |
| Average US day | ~4.5 | ~300 W |
| Northern winter | ~2 | ~700 W or more |

A 200 W panel on an average day produces about 630 Wh, which is roughly 1.5 days of sun for a full recharge ([Watt48](https://www.watt48.com/guides/solar-panel-charging-power-station), [Power Preflight](https://powerpreflight.com/guides/solar-panel-sizing-portable-power-stations/)). The sizing figures come from [Power Runtime](https://power-runtime.pages.dev/guides/solar-charging/) and [Held in Reserve](https://www.heldinreserve.com/power/solar-panels-recharge-power-station/).

**The formula:** panel watts ≈ 1,000 Wh ÷ (peak sun hours × 0.7 to 0.75). The 0.7–0.75 factor accounts for real-world losses from heat, panel angle, and charger electronics. [Enough Watts](https://enoughwatts.com/guides/how-much-solar-to-recharge) uses 0.75, and [Power Preflight](https://powerpreflight.com/guides/solar-panel-sizing-portable-power-stations/) uses 0.7.

**Check these before buying:**
- **Solar input limit:** Your station caps how many watts it accepts. Panels beyond that limit add nothing. Many 1,000 Wh stations accept 400–800 W, so check your model's spec sheet ([Held in Reserve](https://www.heldinreserve.com/power/solar-panels-recharge-power-station/)).
- **Voltage window:** Panels wired in series add voltage. Exceeding the station's maximum can damage it, so confirm the combined open-circuit voltage stays within the input range ([Power Preflight](https://powerpreflight.com/guides/solar-panel-sizing-portable-power-stations/)).
- **Running loads while charging:** If you draw power during the day, net charging is slower. Subtract your load from the solar input ([Held in Reserve](https://www.heldinreserve.com/power/solar-panels-recharge-power-station/)).

For most shoppers, a 200 W panel is a practical starting point for a 1,000 Wh station. Choose 300 W or more if you want a one-day recharge or plan to use the station in cloudy or winter conditions.

# Short answer

For most full-size fridges, a station with about 1,000 Wh covers roughly half a day to a day. About 2,000 Wh gives you a full day with margin. Make sure its surge rating is at least about 1,300 W and that it has pure sine wave output.

## The three numbers to check

1. **Surge watts:** This is the spec that most often fails. The compressor briefly draws about 1,000-2,400 W at startup, depending on fridge size ([BlackBox Supplies](https://www.blackboxsupplies.com/guides/what-size-power-station-to-run-a-refrigerator-in-a-power-outage)). One guide uses 1,300 W as a safe allowance ([powerstations.reviews](https://powerstations.reviews/guides/power-station-for-a-refrigerator)). A station with plenty of Wh can still trip at startup if its surge rating is too low.
2. **Continuous watts:** A full-size fridge runs at about 100-250 W, which almost any 1,000 W-class station handles.
3. **Watt-hours:** The compressor only runs about 30-40% of the time. A typical fridge averages roughly 40-70 W, or about 1,000 Wh per day. Adding inverter losses and headroom brings that to about 1,400 Wh per day ([powerstations.reviews](https://powerstations.reviews/guides/power-station-for-a-refrigerator)).

## Rough sizing

| Outage length | Capacity to look for |
|---|---|
| ~12-16 hours | ~1,000 Wh |
| 24 hours | ~1,400-2,000 Wh |
| 2 days | ~2,800 Wh or more |

# Power station size for a fridge during an outage

Check three numbers: surge watts, continuous watts, and watt-hours.

**1. Surge watts: at least 1,300 W.** The compressor briefly draws about 2-3x its running watts when it starts. Sources put this at roughly 1,000-1,800 W for a typical fridge and up to 2,400 W for large or older ones. Surge is the spec that most often makes a power station fail to run a fridge, not battery size ([BlackBox Supplies](https://www.blackboxsupplies.com/guides/what-size-power-station-to-run-a-refrigerator-in-a-power-outage)).

**2. Continuous watts: about 250 W or more.** A full-size fridge runs at about 100-250 W, so almost any 1,000 W-class unit clears this. Choose a pure sine wave inverter.

**3. Watt-hours: this sets how long it runs.** The compressor cycles on only about a third of the time, so the average draw is around 40-70 W.
- **About 1,000 Wh:** roughly 17 hours ([HomeGrid HQ](https://www.homegridhq.com/watts/fridge/)).
- **About 1,400 Wh:** a full day, including inverter losses and 20% headroom ([powerstations.reviews](https://powerstations.reviews/guides/power-station-for-a-refrigerator)).
- **About 2,000 Wh:** roughly 33 hours.
- **About 2,800 Wh:** two days.

To size it for your own fridge, find the kWh per year on its yellow EnergyGuide label. Then compute: kWh/year × 1,000 ÷ 8,760 = average watts. Multiply that by 24 hours and the number of days, divide by 0.85, then multiply by 1.2.

Keep the door closed during the outage. Food is generally unsafe after about 4 hours above 40°F with the door shut.

## H35
QUESTION: How many watts of solar to recharge a 1000Wh power station?

**Short answer: about 200-300 W of solar panels for a 1,000 Wh power station.** 200 W refills it in about 1.5 days of decent sun, and about 300-350 W does it in one good day.

**The math**

Daily energy = panel watts × peak sun hours × about 0.7-0.75. The 0.7-0.75 covers heat, panel angle and charge-controller losses.

- **200 W panel, average day (4.5 sun hours, 70%):** about 630 Wh/day, so a little over a day and a half to refill from empty ([Watt48](https://www.watt48.com/guides/solar-panel-charging-power-station), [Power Preflight](https://powerpreflight.com/guides/solar-panel-sizing-portable-power-stations/)).
- **One-day recharge:** about 340 W at 4 sun hours, 270 W at 5, and 230 W at 6 ([Power Runtime](https://power-runtime.pages.dev/guides/solar-charging/)).
- **Summer vs. winter:** about 300 W on a good summer day, but 700 W or more in a northern winter ([Held in Reserve](https://www.heldinreserve.com/power/solar-panels-recharge-power-station/)).
- **Rule of thumb:** battery Wh ÷ 5 gives the panel watts that recharge it in about a day and a half, which is 200 W here ([Power Preflight](https://powerpreflight.com/guides/solar-panel-sizing-portable-power-stations/)).

**Before you buy**

- **Solar input limit:** Your station caps how many watts it accepts. A station limited to 200 W won't use a 400 W panel. Mid-range 1,000-1,500 Wh units typically allow 400-800 W ([Held in Reserve](https://www.heldinreserve.com/power/solar-panels-recharge-power-station/)).
- **Voltage:** The panel's open-circuit voltage (Voc) must stay inside your station's input range. Going over can damage the charge controller. Wiring panels in series adds voltage, so parallel is the safer default ([Power Preflight](https://powerpreflight.com/guides/solar-panel-sizing-portable-power-stations/)).
- **Connectors:** Panels usually have MC4 plugs, while stations may use XT60 or DC barrel ports, so check you have the right adapter.
- **Using power while charging:** Running devices during the day slows the net recharge.
- **Clouds and shade:** Solid cloud cuts output to roughly 10-25%, so plan for your worst season.
